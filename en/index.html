Station brief

Start from a concrete scene, then set out the questions, methods and boundaries of this station.

Looking back at 1929, it is easy to ask how people missed it. Go back to 1929 and the data, newspapers and arguments available were incomplete; many people were still watching a boom. Historical work reconstructs what could be known at the time before explaining what happened later.

Sources come in layers: prices, contracts, statutes and ledgers are primary; contemporary newspapers and commentary are secondary; textbooks and later research are a third layer. Mix them and outcomes start to look like causes.

Institutions change mechanisms. The gold standard, capital controls, central-bank tools and war finance all make the same theory produce different results in different decades. Align institutions before comparing numbers.

Historical analogy is useful when it compares mechanisms, not endings. Every bubble, crisis and inflation episode has different participants, leverage, regulation and politics. “It looked like last time” is not analysis by itself.

China and comparative history need extra care. Statistical definitions, borders, price systems and institutional categories keep changing. A word from the 1930s may not mean the same thing today.

After this station, you should be able to take a historical source, separate the primary record from later interpretation, and state the institutional conditions under which its claim holds.

After this station, you should be able to answer

  • Why was the gold standard hard to maintain in wars and crises?
  • Why did the Industrial Revolution begin in Britain?
  • What institutional starting point shaped China’s reform and opening?

Four common misreadings

These claims come up again and again in reader mail, comments and working discussions. Naming them is more useful than repeating definitions.

“History repeats itself.”

What repeats is often a mechanism: leverage, panic, runs, policy response. The ending is not copied, because participants, institutions and outside conditions change. Say which layer matches and which does not.

“A historical source is a fact.”

A source is a record left by someone in some position, with bias, omissions and version problems. Price series may have missing months, conversions and definition changes. Criticise the source before quoting it.

“The gold standard was more stable.”

The gold standard fixed exchange rates but pushed adjustment onto wages, prices and employment. In war, runs and conflicts over policy goals, keeping the convertibility promise could be very costly.

“The Industrial Revolution was one genius’s doing.”

Technology, capital, coal, wages, demand, imperial trade, scientific institutions and property arrangements all interacted. Single-cause stories sound clean but do not survive regional variation and timing.

Can 1929 and 2008 be compared directly?

Three or four articles from this station, applied to one decision. Every number is a teaching assumption for practising the order of judgement, not an estimate of a real market.

Both crises brought falling asset prices, credit contraction and policy responses, but the toolkits differed. In 1929 there was no deposit insurance, the gold standard constrained policy, and automatic fiscal stabilisers were weak; in 2008 central banks could expand balance sheets, deposits were insured, and macro policy was more complete.

What can be compared is the mechanism: leverage, collateral haircuts, runs and policy reaction. What cannot is the institutional toolkit, the position in global supply chains and household balance sheets. Keep the two categories apart or the analogy misleads.

When making the comparison, use three columns: same mechanism, different institutions, missing evidence. If a column is empty, the conclusion is a hypothesis; write “this time may resemble that time if…” rather than “this time will be like that time”.

Read 1929 and 2008 with that frame; it is more useful than memorising how much the market fell in each year. History supplies a list of mechanisms, not a ready-made answer.
