The Formation of Marxist Economics

From the 1848 Manifesto to Capital Volume I in 1867, Marx analysed capitalism as a historical system; this article traces the texts, core arguments, debates and later misreadings.

Problem and scope

“The formation of Marxist economics” refers to the intellectual process running from Marx’s critiques of German philosophy, French socialism and British political economy in the 1840s to the publication of the first volume of Capital in 1867. This article asks how he analysed capitalism as a historical system, how he explained value and exploitation, and which claims belong to Marx’s texts rather than to later “Marxism.” It is not a history of parties, and it does not treat Soviet or contemporary policy as Marx’s own programme.

Background

Industrialisation, working-class poverty and cyclical crises are the cluster of problems analysed in the Manifesto and Capital; they are not independent statistical findings asserted by this article [1][4]. Marx studied political economy in London, and Hegelian dialectic, Feuerbach's materialism and French socialism were interlocutors [5]. These sources did not combine automatically into a system; this article follows textual differences rather than assuming a one-way construction.

Primary texts

The Communist Manifesto (1848, with Engels) is a political programme. It presents recorded history as a history of class struggles, stresses the bourgeoisie’s capacity to transform production, and argues that the bourgeoisie creates its own opposition. It is not a summary of Capital.

The preface to A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy (1859) places the relation between social existence and consciousness at the centre of historical analysis and sets out the framework of productive forces and relations of production. The main text begins with the use-value and exchange-value of commodities.[3].

Capital, Volume I (1867) distinguishes labour from labour power: the capitalist buys the capacity to work, not completed labour. Surplus value arises from the part of the working day that exceeds necessary labour [3][4]. Marx treats classical political economy as a tradition that uncovered essential relations, while criticising “vulgar political economy” for describing only surface appearances [3]. The first German edition appeared in Hamburg in 1867; the English translation used here was edited by Engels and published in 1887 [4].

Main debates

First, can the labour theory of value explain prices? Critics pose the “transformation problem” and question the move from values to prices of production, and Marx's own attempt runs into technical difficulties; defenders stress that it is an analysis of social relations [3]. Second, is historical materialism economic determinism? The texts stress the constraints of production, but later interpretations differ sharply over the weight of “base” and “superstructure” [3]. Third, is surplus value a normative judgment or an empirical mechanism? It describes exploitation and also needs to be checked against wages and profits [3][4]. Fourth, what is the relation between Marx and “Marxism”? Engels edited Volumes II and III of Capital after Marx's death, and later party programmes, the Soviet model and Western Marxism are still less identical with his texts [6].

Legacy

Class analysis, crisis theory, alienation and inequality research continue to influence economics and social theory. In the mid-to-late nineteenth century, Marxism helped consolidate, inspire and radicalise labour and socialist movements in western Europe, and a more moderate social democracy later emerged from that current [6]; the labour theory of value, by contrast, remains a subject of dispute rather than a generally accepted theory of price [3].

Hindsight

Treating Capital as a manual for predicting collapse, or treating 1917 or planned economies as Marx’s design, reads later outcomes back into the texts as necessary conclusions. Conversely, reducing Marx to philosophy without economic analysis ignores his sustained work on value, the working day and capital.

Knowledge-point review

background of Marxist economics

Definition: Marxist economics formed amid 19th-century industrial capitalism, worker movements, German philosophy, French socialism and British political economy.

Mechanism: Hegelian dialectics, Feuerbach's materialism, Owen and Fourier's utopian socialism and Ricardo's economics shaped Marx.

Example: For example, factory conditions and inequality prompted his analysis of capital accumulation and labour relations.

Boundary: Background is not one cause; Marx both inherited and critiqued classical economics, combining moral critique with structural analysis, not mere anger or economic determinism.

major works

Definition: Marx's major works include the Communist Manifesto (1848), Capital (1867) and the Grundrisse.

Mechanism: The Manifesto states the programme of class struggle and historical materialism, while Capital analyses commodities, surplus value, accumulation and crises.

Example: For example, Capital begins with the dual nature of the commodity and develops the labour theory of value and surplus value.

Boundary: The works are not fixed; Marx left later volumes unfinished, manuscripts and Engels's editing differ, and early and late ideas developed, so fragments are insufficient.

different strands of development

Definition: Marxist economics later diversified into social democracy, Leninism, Trotskyism, Western Marxism, dependency theory and analytical Marxism.

Mechanism: Strands differ on revolution, the state, imperialism, class and planning; theory mixed with labour movements, colonial experience and academic research.

Example: For example, Lenin analysed imperialism and monopoly capital, Gramsci stressed culture and hegemony, and dependency theory examined core-periphery structures.

Boundary: Strands are not simply orthodox versus heretical; political practice, history and competition shaped differences, and the same label can cover conflicts.

theoretical debates and influence

Definition: Debates in Marxist economics include the labour theory of value, surplus value, falling profit rates, crisis theory, class structure and the state.

Mechanism: Supporters stress capital relations, exploitation and crises; critics point to value calculation, institutional variety and prediction problems; influence extends to history, sociology and development studies.

Example: For example, the falling-rate debate turns on technical composition, wages and counter-trends.

Boundary: Theory is not a prophecy machine; some predictions did not unfold as stated, versions and interpretations vary, and critique and inheritance must separate political labels from testable claims.

Exercises

  1. How do the Manifesto and Capital differ in their mode of argument, and why can neither replace the other?

  2. Why does the distinction between labour and labour power matter for the theory of surplus value?

  3. What qualifications does the claim “Marx thought the economy determines everything” require?

  4. Two views of capitalism. Set side by side the claims that capitalism is a historical institution and that it is the permanent natural order of human society. What evidence would each view lead a reader to look for? Which one relies more on the analysis of the commodity, value and the circulation of capital in Capital, and which is closer to the political narrative the Manifesto addresses?

Suggested answer points

  1. The Manifesto is a political programme that uses historical narrative and political appeal to set out its goals; Capital analyses commodities, value and the circulation of capital systematically. They share questions but differ in evidence and level of argument.

  2. The capitalist buys the right to direct labour power for a period, not labour already performed. Labour power can create more value than its wage, and that difference is the source of surplus value; conflating the two leaves profit unexplained.

  3. The texts stress the constraints that production places on politics, law and ideas, but do not reduce every cultural phenomenon to economics. Later one-way readings of “base and superstructure” are contested. Distinguish Marx's formulations, Engels's work as editor of Volumes II and III after Marx's death, and later textbooks [3][6].

  4. Treating it as a historical institution makes a reader ask how it arose, what conditions it depends on and how it might change - the path closer to Capital's commodity-value-circulation analysis. Treating it as a permanent natural order tends to take competition and private arrangements as constants of human nature and asks fewer questions about conditions; that reading is closer to the political narrative the Manifesto argues against and to later textbook simplifications. The same facts yield different explanations, so the difference has to be drawn at the level of the texts rather than by slogan.
