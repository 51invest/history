Problem and scope

This article examines the succession of international monetary regimes from the late nineteenth century to the 1970s: the classical gold standard, the Bretton Woods peg and floating rates. It does not cover exchange-rate forecasting or optimum currency areas; the focus is on rules, convertibility and adjustment.

Background

Under the gold standard, countries fixed their currencies' gold content and promised conversion at a fixed parity, so exchange rates followed gold parities. Adjustment ran through gold flows, wages and prices to output (Bordo 2006). Fixed rates and capital mobility compress monetary autonomy; this trilemma is the article's analytical frame, not a measured claim about any particular period. After World War I the classical gold standard broke down; interwar competitive devaluations and trade restrictions deepened the Depression (Federal Reserve History).

Primary materials

Conference records, the IMF Articles, central bank archives, government announcements and exchange-rate statistics are the main materials. The article uses three Federal Reserve History essays for the timeline and checks them against an official IMF history chapter, Bordo's institutional history and the 1971 Federal Register text. Negotiation details still require archives; the key facts no longer rest on a single institution.

Change

In July 1944, delegates from 44 nations at Bretton Woods agreed to create the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development. Currencies were fixed but adjustable against the dollar within a 1 percent band; the dollar was convertible into gold at $35 an ounce. The IMF formally began in December 1945 with 29 members; the system became fully functional in 1958 (Federal Reserve History).

As U.S. deficits widened, foreign dollar claims rose; the source states verbatim, "By 1961, however, the amount of dollar claims outstanding began to exceed the U.S. government's stock of gold," so the comparison is outstanding dollar claims against the U.S. government's gold stock (Federal Reserve History). On the evening of 15 August 1971 Nixon announced the closing of the gold window; the IMF's official history records that the United States informed the Fund that day that it would no longer freely buy and sell gold, suspending official dollar convertibility (IMF 1996). Executive Order 11615, signed the same day, froze prices, rents, wages and salaries for 90 days from 15 August, with ceilings set by the highest actual transactions in the 30 days ending 14 August and with raw agricultural products exempt; Proclamation 4074 imposed a 10 percent supplemental duty on dutiable imports entered or withdrawn for consumption after 12:01 a.m. on 16 August, and the proclamation itself took effect at that time (Federal Register 1971). The announcement and the freeze therefore start on 15 August, the import surcharge takes effect at 12:01 a.m. on 16 August, and the gold-window suspension is dated by the notification to the Fund that day (Federal Reserve History; IMF 1996).

In December 1971 leading developed countries reached the Smithsonian Agreement: the dollar was devalued about 8.5 percent against gold to $38 an ounce, and about 10.7 percent on average against key currencies. Within fifteen months the Bretton Woods system collapsed and major currencies moved to floating (Federal Reserve History; IMF 1996). A note on the arithmetic: raising the gold price from $35 to $38 is an increase of 8.6 percent, which is the same as the dollar losing 7.9 percent of its gold content ($1/38 against $1/35); the source describes the move as an approximately 8.5 percent devaluation, and that wording is reproduced here with both calculations shown. The same essay adds that on 12 February 1973 the United States devalued the dollar by an additional 10 percent to $42 an ounce, that "within a month nearly all major currencies were floating against the dollar" and that "the Bretton Woods system was finished", citing IMF 1973, 2-8 (Federal Reserve History), which matches the collapse within fifteen months described above.

Debates

First, did the gold standard provide a stable, credible nominal anchor, or did it turn adjustment into deflation and unemployment? Bordo sets the smooth adjustment of the classical gold standard against the misaligned parities, deflation and weak credibility of the interwar gold standard, citing Eichengreen (1992) on the latter's fragility (Bordo 2006). Second, Bretton Woods used capital controls and adjustable pegs to restrict capital flows and faced the Triffin dilemma: the United States had to supply dollar reserves, which undermined confidence in dollar-gold convertibility (Bordo 2006). Third, floating rates brought policy autonomy and volatility; whether they impose a stronger inflation discipline is a question for cross-country evidence. Fourth, 1971 was not inevitable. Bordo records McKinnon, Meltzer and others arguing that the system could have continued as a de facto dollar standard; Bordo himself places French gold conversion alongside the expansionary U.S. monetary and fiscal policies from 1965 that financed the Vietnam War and the Great Society (Bordo 2006). Federal Reserve History lists the deteriorating balance of payments, military spending and foreign aid (Federal Reserve History); the IMF's official history lists persistent deficits, gold losses and accumulated foreign dollar claims (IMF 1996). The accounts overlap without being identical, and the weight of each factor is a historical debate that this article does not estimate.

Legacy

The IMF and World Bank survive; the dollar remains the main reserve and settlement currency; floating rates are the norm, though countries still intervene in and manage their exchange rates. Gold no longer determines money's value but remains a reserve asset.

Hindsight

The 1944 designers did not plan the 1971 collapse; reading Bretton Woods as doomed, or floating rates as superior, ignores the constraints of the time and later performance.

Boundary case: A gold parity of 35 dollars an ounce after a 30% devaluation

With a gold parity of 35 dollars an ounce and a currency fixed at 4.86 per dollar, a 30% official devaluation gives a new parity of about 4.86 ÷ 0.70 = 6.94; a 15% devaluation gives 5.72. If reserves are 10 billion dollars and 3 billion flow out, coverage falls from 100% to 70%; if 6 billion flow out, coverage is 40% and defending the parity becomes more costly.

The conclusion reverses when the market believes the adjustment is temporary: if capital returns and exports improve, lower reserves need not force abandonment; if wages and prices rise with devaluation, the real exchange rate may return to its starting point. The boundary is that the gold standard, Bretton Woods and floating rates are three adjustment mechanisms. Fixed rates press adjustment into wages and prices; floating rates put it into the nominal exchange rate, with different costs.

  2. Deficits raised foreign dollar claims while U.S. gold was limited, so conversion expectations created run pressure.
  3. Not necessarily; capital flows, inflation and exchange-rate pass-through can constrain policy.
