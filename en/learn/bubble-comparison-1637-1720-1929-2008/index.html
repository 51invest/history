Comparing Bubbles: 1637, 1720, 1929 and 2008

Four famous bubbles differ in assets, leverage and institutions; compare mechanisms, not overlaid price curves.

ModuleHIS-M16 · 泡沫危机与事件
LevelSystematic
Read7 min · 20 sections

Definition

A bubble usually means an asset price far above what sustainable cash flows or fundamentals can explain, sustained by later buyers paying more. It is not any price increase: new technology, lower rates and higher earnings can justify higher prices. A bubble comparison should separate asset type, funding, participants, narrative and policy response, not just percentage gains. Hindsight bias is overestimating how predictable an event was once the outcome is known [3].

Intuition

Bubbles are four patients with fever: similar symptoms, different causes. The 1637 tulip mania lacked durable cash flow; the 1720 South Sea and Mississippi schemes relied on sovereign debt conversion and charter stories; 1929 equities relied on margin and leverage; 2008 relied on mortgage securitisation and short-term funding. The common elements are prices detached from fundamentals, leverage amplifying moves and narratives attracting late buyers; the differences are the asset, leverage channel, regulation and ultimate bag holder. Comparing bubbles is not finding a universal formula but identifying which mechanisms repeat and which conditions are unique.

Mechanism

The 1637 Dutch tulip mania was mainly spot bulbs and forward contracts with a relatively narrow base; in 1720 the South Sea and Mississippi schemes bundled sovereign debt, shares and colonial-trade expectations, with credit and sovereign endorsement amplifying leverage; in 1929 the US stock market combined margin loans, leveraged trusts and rising inequality, and the crash transmitted to banks; in 2008 housing mortgages, securitisation and short-term wholesale funding transmitted losses through shadow banks and repo. All four featured a 'this time is different' narrative, but the source of leverage and the final bearer differed [1][2].

Worked example

Use a comparison table rather than one price chart: asset (bulbs/shares/shares/housing), funding (spot and forwards/sovereign debt and shares/margin loans/securitisation and repo), participants (merchants/aristocrats and investors/retail and trusts/global banks), narrative (scarcity and the exotic/debt resolution/permanent prosperity/house prices never fall), policy (ex-post bans/rescue and compensation/rate rises and tariffs/rate cuts and bailouts). Suppose an investor buys USD 100 of stock in 1929 with 50% margin; if the price falls 33%, equity falls from USD 50 to USD 17, a 66% loss, while an unlevered portfolio loses 33%. Leverage magnifies the same price move, but the source of leverage differed across episodes. These are teaching assumptions.

Second worked example: how leverage amplifies losses

Suppose an asset costs CNY 100, the investor puts in CNY 10 and borrows CNY 90 — 10x leverage. A 10% fall to CNY 90 wipes out the equity; a 15% fall to CNY 85 leaves equity at −CNY 5 and triggers margin calls. At 2x leverage (CNY 50 equity, CNY 50 debt), a 30% fall to CNY 70 still leaves CNY 20 of equity; for the same decline, the highly leveraged holder is already wiped out. The damage from a bursting bubble depends not only on the price fall but on the financing structure: margin loans, repo, short-term paper and structured products trigger margin calls and forced sales, pushing prices lower. These are teaching assumptions and exclude fees and tax.

Boundary and common mistakes

First, a price rise is not necessarily a bubble; without evidence on cash flows, leverage and the buyer chain, gains alone are insufficient. Second, a bubble may not crash immediately; it can stagnate for years or be eroded by inflation. Third, ex-post narratives selectively preserve early warnings and ignore contemporaneous disagreement. Fourth, institutional differences across assets and markets are large, and overlaying price curves misleads. Fifth, comparison is not for predicting the next crash but for identifying common vulnerabilities in leverage, narrative and regulation.

Failure modes

The first failure is hindsight bias: after the fact the bubble is obvious; at the time, participants saw rising prices, peer returns and a 'this time is different' narrative. The second is sample selection: picking four famous crashes while ignoring periods that overheated without crashing overstates 'bubbles always burst'. The third is inconsistent data: assets, leverage and market size across centuries are not directly comparable, and price indices may cover only a few trades. The fourth is correlation as causation: low rates, leverage and narrative appearing together does not make one the sole cause. The fifth is ignoring policy response: 1929 and 2008 differed in monetary policy, fiscal rescue and regulatory reform, and so did the outcomes.

Three checks

First, from what fundamentals did the price detach: cash flow, rent, dividends or collateral value, and how large is the gap? Second, where did leverage come from: margin, repo, short-term paper or tranching, and who faces margin calls on the way down? Third, how did the narrative reinforce itself: why did new entrants believe 'this time is different', and had the exit already narrowed? Beyond these, ask 'if it does not crash, how would I know?' — avoid inferring only from crash samples.

Adjacent concepts and a counterexample

Bubbles are easily confused with manias, crises and crashes. A mania emphasises sentiment and narrative, a crisis emphasises systemic financial disruption, a crash emphasises a rapid price fall; a bubble is a self-reinforcing process in which price detaches from fundamentals and depends on later buyers. They overlap but are not synonyms. A counterexample is 'a price rise is a bubble': prices can rise on earnings, scarcity or policy change, and a bubble claim is justified only when we can say which fundamental the price left, how leverage amplified it and how the exit narrowed. Another is 'no crash means no bubble': a bubble can deflate slowly without crashing, and using crash as the only criterion misses overvaluation and misallocation. Compare bubble episodes on valuation, leverage, narrative, regulation and policy response together.

Scope and limits

This bubble-comparison framework applies to historical episodes with price series, leverage and financing data, and traceable narratives and policy responses. If price data are missing, asset definitions change, or leverage comes from informal channels, cross-episode comparison is distorted. It does not apply to inferring a pre-crash bubble from the crash itself, nor to treating different assets and institutions as one mechanism. Align real prices, leverage and exit conditions, then test counterfactuals and alternative explanations; compare mechanisms, not moral judgments.

Background

The 1637 tulip episode, the 1720 South Sea and Mississippi schemes, the 1929 US stock crash and the 2008 housing crisis are often compared. They share leverage, narrative and liquidity interactions, but the assets, institutions, monetary conditions and participant structures differ.

Information available

Available information includes contemporaneous contracts and price records, newspapers and parliamentary papers, later statistical reconstructions and scholarship. Early episodes are incompletely recorded, and price series and asset definitions are contested; a single “high price” is not the whole market.

Outcome

All four episodes saw rapid price rises, leverage expansion and sharp reversals; the triggers, duration, policy response and real-economy damage differed greatly. 1929 and 2008 have fuller macro data; 1637 and 1720 rely more on fragmented evidence.

Alternative explanations

Beyond “crowd irrationality”, credit conditions, regulatory changes, information asymmetry, wealth effects and international capital flows can explain the episodes. Calling every one a “bubble” hides institutional differences and lets hindsight replace evidence.

Limits to generalization

Do not convert 1637 tulip prices directly into modern returns, or apply 2008 policy tools to 1720. Comparisons must rebuild prices, leverage and losses on a common basis and state which variables are missing.
