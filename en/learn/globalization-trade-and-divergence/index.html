Globalization, Trade and Divergence

From Ricardo to twenty-first-century trade openness, this article examines how globalization widened and then partly narrowed income gaps between countries, separating theoretical gains from historical distribution.

Problem and scope

This article examines globalization, trade and divergence since 1820, focusing on comparative advantage, openness and the "Great Divergence". It does not present globalization as linear progress or attribute today's income gaps entirely to trade.

Background

In the nineteenth century, steamships, railways, the telegraph and the gold standard cut transport and information costs; Europe exported capital and labour, and trade expanded. The first wave reversed in the world wars and the Great Depression. After 1945, tariff cuts, containerisation and global value chains supported another expansion; growth slowed after 2008. This paragraph is the article's own historical summary: neither source retrieved this round - the World Bank trade-ratio series and Maddison 2023 - covers pre-1960 trade shares or quantifies a "reversal", so it carries no citation marker.

Primary materials

Ricardo's Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817) used Portuguese wine and English cloth to explain comparative advantage: even a country less efficient in every product gains by specialising according to relative opportunity cost. It is a theory with assumptions, not a full description of real trade. This paragraph restates the argument in standard textbook terms and checks it against no edition: the Econlib electronic text registered earlier could not be retrieved here (curl 403 with a 1,662-byte block page; rendered-browser navigation times out at 30 seconds), so nothing can be said about wording differences between the 1817 edition and that electronic version, and that source has been removed from the list.

World Bank data show world trade (exports plus imports) rising from about 26% of GDP in 1970 to about 60% in 2008, falling to 52% in 2009, and standing at about 62% in 2022 and 57% in 2024. The ratio uses current US dollars and is affected by exchange rates and GDP structure.

The Maddison Project Database 2023 (Bolt and van Zanden, Groningen Growth and Development Centre) publishes a "GDPpc" sheet labelled "GDP pc 2011 prices" - per-capita output in 2011 international dollars at purchasing power parity. Dividing the United Kingdom (GBR) column by China (CHN) and India (IND) inside that same sheet gives, in the table's own values: UK/China 3,306/882 = 3.75 in 1820, 11,061/799 = 13.84 in 1950 and 38,407/19,238 = 2.00 in 2022; UK/India 5,829/850 = 6.86 in 1870, 11,061/987 = 11.21 in 1950 and 38,407/7,766 = 4.95 in 2022[2]. The sheet has no India value for 1820, so the India series starts in 1870. Its "Sources" sheet states that "GDP pc: 2008 - 2022: Total Economy Database the Conference Board for all countries included in TED. Otherwise UN national accounts statistics" - recent years therefore rest on a third-party compilation rather on Maddison's own estimates. These are historical PPP estimates, not precise measurements.

Main debates

First, is trade a cause or a consequence of growth? Openness and growth are correlated, but geography, institutions and policy affect both, complicating causal identification. Second, why did the Great Divergence occur? Geography, institutions, colonialism, technology and energy all have evidence; no single explanation suffices. Third, has globalization reduced gaps? China and other East Asian economies narrowed the gap with rich countries, while sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Latin America fell behind - the first half of this claim is checkable in the Maddison sheet cited under Change, the regional lag is the article's own summary of regional series, not computed this round; convergence between countries and within-country inequality can coexist. Fourth, is comparative advantage static? Dynamic advantages, learning and industrial policy can change the long-run path of specialisation. Fifth, has there been "deglobalization" since 2008? The trade-to-GDP ratio has flattened, but services, data and regional trade keep changing; goods trade alone is an incomplete measure.

Legacy

Globalization raised specialisation and knowledge diffusion, while bringing import competition, relocation, financial contagion and supply-chain fragility. Export-oriented industrialisation, value chains and multilateral rules changed developing countries' growth paths; tariffs, subsidies and geopolitics now rebalance "efficiency first".

Hindsight

Do not read today's rich-country list back into the nineteenth century as proof of inevitable success, nor treat "openness" as a universal medicine. Nineteenth-century globalization came with colonialism and unequal treaties; late developers often combined protection, education and industrial policy. Trade theory describes potential gains, not guaranteed fair distribution or automatic results.

Boundary case: A ten-point rise in trade share may leave only 0.5 of net gain

Suppose a country's trade-to-GDP ratio rises from 20% to 30%, and openness lifts annual growth from 2% to 4%, but adjustment costs equal 1.5% of GDP. If the gain lasts ten years, cumulative extra growth is about 20 percentage points, and net gain survives the adjustment cost; if adjustment costs are 3.5%, the net gain disappears. If only the top 10% capture the gain while the other 90% bear job losses and price shocks, average national income rises while households may feel worse off.

The conclusion reverses when openness comes with institutions and education: if ports, customs, contracts and skills do not keep up, a higher trade share need not produce growth. The boundary is that comparative advantage explains the theoretical total gain, not who receives it or how quickly. Using national average income instead of distribution erases winners and losers.

Exercises

  1. Use Ricardo's example to distinguish comparative from absolute advantage and state the assumptions.
  2. Does a falling trade-to-GDP ratio mean globalization is reversing? What else explains it?
  3. In explanations of the Great Divergence, what are geography's and institutions' strengths and weaknesses?
Suggested answer points
  1. Absolute advantage compares output per unit of input; comparative advantage compares opportunity cost. Even if one side is more efficient in both goods, specialising by lower opportunity cost is mutually beneficial. Assumptions include perfect competition, immobile factors and zero transport costs (this list is the article's textbook summary, not wording checked against an edition).
  2. Not necessarily. Changes in GDP, exchange rates, prices and economic structure affect the ratio; trade may shift to services, regional partners or intermediate goods while volumes grow.
  3. Geography stresses climate, disease, resources and transport; institutions stress property rights, colonial legacies and political constraints. Both explain some cases but face endogeneity and measurement problems.
