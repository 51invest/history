Agriculture, Commerce and Early Markets

From Domesday (1086) to eighteenth-century agricultural commercialisation, this article examines how farming, trade and market institutions evolved in Europe, mainly England, and where continuity and rupture lie.

Problem and scope

This article covers European agriculture, commerce and early markets from about 1000 to 1800, mainly in England. It explains how exchange expanded from local markets to interregional trade. It does not explain the Industrial Revolution itself, and it does not present marketisation as a single, inevitable path.

Background

Two terms are used in this article. Open fields refers to the arrangement in which arable land lay in scattered strips, with fallow and grazing governed by village custom; market integration refers to how far local prices converged through trade and arbitrage, usually measured by price gaps and correlations. No figures are given here, only what to look at.

Medieval Europe was overwhelmingly agricultural; the manor bound peasants to the land and to their lord, and manorial custom governed production and exchange [5]. Most people farmed small plots with a limited surplus; transport was costly and weights, measures and coinage varied. From the eleventh century, a revival of commerce and the growth of towns expanded local fairs and long-distance trade [5].

Primary materials

Domesday Book (1086) surveyed land and property after the Norman Conquest. It recorded lords, tenants, ploughs, dependent populations and land values, using fiscal units such as hides, and compared values before and after 1066. The Patcham entry in Sussex lists 163 villagers, 45 smallholders and 82 ploughs. It describes almost all of England and names more than 13,000 places, but London, Winchester, County Durham and Northumberland were left out [3][4]. It is a land and property record compiled for royal taxation and land management; Encyclopaedia Britannica describes it as a survey of land and landholdings, not a population census, so this article does not read a population total directly from it [4].

Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations (1776) argued that the division of labour is limited by the extent of the market and treated exchange as a feature of commercial society. It is analysis, not statistical evidence; the "invisible hand" is not a blanket endorsement of market outcomes.

The Bank of England's "A millennium of macroeconomic data" (version 3.1, updated to 2016, with most series reaching back to the thirteenth century and one or two benchmark estimates for 1086) provides long series for population, real output, employment and real wages. It can test the narrative, but its estimates rest on models and assumptions. Coverage varies by series: the early population figures are for England, eighteenth-century employment for Great Britain, and the real consumption earnings index is set to 1900 = 100 using English daily wage rates before 1750.

Main debates

First, was the medieval economy a "natural economy" or already highly commercialised? Local and long-distance trade existed, but most production remained organised around household and manor. Second, did living standards rise after the Black Death? Population collapse produced labour shortages, and some surviving tenants obtained better wages and rents; but mortality, institutions and demographic recovery differed by region, so a rise in output per head is not by itself an improvement in living standards [6]. Third, was agricultural improvement progress or dispossession? Enclosure consolidated scattered communal strips into individually owned and managed plots, changing common rights [7]; efficiency and distribution are separate questions. Fourth, was Smith describing or prescribing? He analysed gains from exchange but also criticised merchants and governments, and cannot be reduced to "markets always work".

Legacy

This article gives one sourced example rather than claiming that all early markets followed one path; the mechanisms still require account rolls, court records and price series.

Early markets left an institutional legacy: in sixteenth-century Europe, negotiable bills of exchange, bills obligatory and government annuities developed alongside legally enforceable transactions, extending credit across time and place [8]. But market expansion was entangled with rules, power and exclusion; projecting today's property institutions back into the Middle Ages obscures their local and hierarchical character.

Hindsight

Looking back from the Industrial Revolution, it is tempting to present agricultural commercialisation as a necessary "run-up" to take-off. Many regions remained in low-output equilibria, and commercial prosperity could be interrupted by war, plague or shifting trade routes. Smith's judgement belongs to the eighteenth century; it was not a prediction of later growth.

Knowledge-point review

agriculture and cities

Definition: Agricultural surplus supports cities, specialisation and states, while cities provide markets, administration and military functions.

Mechanism: Irrigation, crop domestication, land tenure and taxation determine surplus; urban density brings innovation but also disease, fire and congestion.

Example: For example, Mesopotamian irrigation raised yields and supported city-states, temples and redistribution.

Boundary: Agriculture is not one-way progress; intensification can bring soil degradation, inequality and disease, and cities depend on rural surplus and fragile supply chains.

trade and commerce

Definition: Trade and commerce expand markets through regional differences, routes, commercial law and credit networks, with merchants, guilds and chartered companies as key organisations.

Mechanism: Trade is limited by transport, tariffs, money, war and information; the commercial revolution brought accounting, insurance, bills and capital markets.

Example: For example, the Silk Road linked Eurasian goods and ideas, and Mediterranean merchants used bills and partnerships to share risk.

Boundary: Trade is not natural growth; violence, colonialism and monopoly shaped routes, merchants rent-seeked, and commercial gains were unevenly shared, so volume alone is insufficient.

colonialism

Definition: Colonialism is control of other lands and peoples through conquest, settlement, trade monopoly and imposed institutions, reshaping economies and the global division of labour.

Mechanism: It extracted resources through land seizure, forced labour, plantations, taxes and monetary integration, spread law, technology and disease, and left institutional legacies after independence.

Example: For example, American plantations and silver mining linked Europe while causing demographic collapse and land concentration.

Boundary: Colonialism was not a gift of modernity; violence, exploitation and extractive institutions created long-term inequality, and infrastructure legacies are contested, not just growth numbers.

industrialisation

Definition: Industrialisation replaced hand and household production with machines, factories and energy, raising productivity, urbanisation and structural change.

Mechanism: Britain industrialised first through coal, steam, textiles, railways and colonial markets, with property rights, science, wages and energy prices as conditions.

Example: For example, cotton mills concentrated production with steam engines, and railways cut transport costs and expanded markets.

Boundary: Industrialisation is not one cause; institutions, resources, demand and global extraction interacted, early factories were harsh, and latecomers followed different paths, so Britain cannot be copied simply.

technology in economic history

Definition: Technology is change in knowledge, tools and processes, and general-purpose technologies such as steam, electricity and computing diffuse widely to reshape production and society.

Mechanism: Innovation depends on science, patents, capital, skills and demand; diffusion lags invention, and complementary investment and organisation determine productivity.

Example: For example, after electricity was invented, factories needed re-layout and training before efficiency gains appeared.

Boundary: Technology is not automatically good; skill bias, unemployment, pollution and concentration create distributional conflict, and lock-in can block better options.

population history

Definition: Population history studies births, deaths, migration and age structure and their relation to resources, technology, institutions and growth.

Mechanism: Malthusian mechanisms, demographic transition, plagues and migration shape labour supply and demand; demographic dividends change with age structure.

Example: For example, the Black Death reduced population and raised wages, and in demographic transition fertility falls before mortality.

Boundary: Population is not simply burden or dividend; technology, institutions and gender roles mediate, forecasts are uncertain, and ageing brings care and fiscal pressure.

globalisation and divergence

Definition: Globalisation is cross-border integration of goods, capital, labour and information, and divergence is the widening or convergence of income and living standards across countries.

Mechanism: 19th-century transport and free trade drove globalisation and a Great Divergence between industrial and primary-exporting countries; some emerging economies converged late 20th century.

Example: For example, steamships and telegraph cut trade costs, but colonies were forced into primary products and limited industrial capability.

Boundary: Globalisation is not evenly beneficial; power, colonialism, tariffs and crises distribute gains, divergence is not destiny, institutions and policy can change paths, but convergence is not universal.

Exercises

  1. Can Domesday Book support a claim of 1.7 million people in England in 1086? What else is needed?
  2. If output per head rose after the Black Death, did survivors necessarily live better? Distinguish output per head, distribution and real wages.
  3. What evidence supports Smith's claim that the division of labour is limited by market extent in eighteenth-century England? What are its limits?
Suggested answer points
  1. Not directly. It records land and property units; population must be estimated separately. The Bank of England's 1.71 million figure also depends on model and coverage assumptions.
  2. Not necessarily. Output per head may rise because population fell; if rents, taxes or prices move against workers, real wages need not improve.
  3. Towns and colonial trade widened markets and encouraged specialisation, but transport costs and regulation still limited market extent. Smith's observation is qualitative, not a causal test.
