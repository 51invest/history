From Aristotle to Aquinas: Wealth, Interest and the Boundaries of Exchange

Early economic thought was not “ancient economics”; it discussed whether exchange is just, money is natural and interest is legitimate within the frames of household, city and salvation. Aristotle separated livelihood from unlimited acquisition; Aquinas placed usury, the just price and fraud inside medieval theology and law.

Problem and scope

This article covers Mediterranean-European thought on wealth, exchange and interest from the fourth century BCE to the thirteenth century CE, focusing on Aristotle’s Politics, Book I, and Aquinas’s Summa Theologiae, II-II, questions 77–78. Chinese, Indian and Islamic traditions also contain rich ideas about wealth and interest; they are mentioned only for comparison and are not forced into one teleological line[1].

Background

Aristotle faced a city-state economy: households needed use values, and market exchange filled gaps. Once money appeared, exchange could cross time and place, and “money breeding money” appeared. Aquinas faced the commercial revival of the thirteenth century: trade, credit and long-distance commerce were expanding, and canon law had to separate commercial profit, interest and fraud[2].

Primary materials and editions

The Aristotle text is the Jowett English translation of Politics, Book I; Aquinas is Summa Theologiae, II-II, questions 77–78, on fraud in buying and selling and on usury. Neither is a modern economics paper: their purposes are city governance and theological ethics, so a sentence cannot be lifted directly into modern policy[3].

Change and timeline

Aristotle separated household management from chrematistics: the former aims at use value and has a limit, the latter seeks money without limit. He treated money exchange as convenience but called usury the least natural form of acquisition, because money exists for exchange and should not “breed” more money[1].

Aquinas inherited and modified this line. He placed commutative justice in equivalence: a seller must not hide defects or exploit a buyer’s urgent need; a usurer sells the use of money, while in scholastic law the use of money was treated as inseparable from the money itself, so interest lacked a just basis[2]. Medieval practice was not a total ban: compensation for loss and risk could be discussed as “extrinsic” titles, leaving room for commercial practice.

Debates

The first debate is what determines the just price: cost, utility, social rank or common estimation? The second is the scope of the usury ban: all interest, or only interest without a justifying title? The third is interpretation: reading Aristotle as “anti-market” or as “anti-unlimited acquisition” produces very different conclusions[3].

Legacy and influence

This line shaped medieval just-price theory, canon-law usury rules and later moral debates about interest. It did not produce modern finance directly, but it raised three questions still in use: is exchange information-symmetric, does price exploit urgency, and does financial return correspond to real risk and service?

Hindsight and analogy

Calling Aristotle “anti-finance” or Aquinas a “precursor of modern interest theory” is hindsight bias. A safer reading is that each answered “what makes exchange just” within his own institutional and theological frame, not that he predicted today’s bond market. Using ancient texts for modern markets requires at least edition, context and purpose checks.

Exercises

  1. Text question: why did Aristotle call usury the “least natural” acquisition, and how does his reason conflict with “interest as risk compensation”?
  2. Institution question: which “extrinsic titles” could leave room for interest within Aquinas’s usury ban? List two and state their conditions.
  3. Interpretation question: why is it an overread to use one sentence from Politics to prove “the ancients opposed markets”? Answer through edition, context and purpose.
