The Keynesian Revolution and Postwar Debates

From the monetary debates of the 1920s through the General Theory, the Employment Act of 1946, the Phillips curve and stagflation, this article examines the texts, policies and disputes of the Keynesian revolution, distinguishing Keynes himself from later "Keynesianism".

Problem and scope

This article examines British and American macroeconomic ideas and policy from the 1920s to the 1980s: how Keynes moved toward "effective demand", how his arguments were absorbed into postwar policy, and how monetarism, rational expectations and stagflation challenged them. It does not treat the "Keynesian revolution" as a single completed event, nor does it cover policy after 2008.

Background

In April 1925, Britain returned to gold at the pre-war parity; an overvalued pound shifted the adjustment burden onto domestic wages and prices, and Keynes argued that this would force deflation [4]. The classical and new classical traditions trusted price adjustment to clear markets [2]; the Great Depression later challenged that confidence [3]. In A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923), Keynes had already rejected restoring the old parity by deflation and wrote that a “managed” currency had become inevitable [1]. These texts were not yet a theory of fiscal stimulus, but they show his concern with short-run stability.

Primary texts

A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923) discusses the quantity of money and the redistribution caused by inflation and deflation, and leaves behind the line “in the long run we are all dead” [1][2]. It rejects restoring the pre-war parity by deflation and argues for managed money, supporting short-run stability rather than the full framework of 1936 [1].

The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936) argued that effective demand could be deficient and that an economy could settle below full employment; aggregate demand has its greatest short-run effect on output and employment, the multiplier is greater than zero, and prices and wages adjust slowly [2]. This summary follows Blinder's scholarly entry; the article does not quote the original. Equating the General Theory with "government spending" is a later simplification.

The US Employment Act of 1946 made it a federal responsibility "to promote maximum employment, production, and purchasing power" and provided for greater coordination between fiscal and monetary policies [3]. It marks the entry of Keynesian ideas into institutions, not a test of one theory.

Main debates

First, was the General Theory a revolution or a systematisation? Keynes drew a clear line against "classical" economics, but whether it was a revolution remains an interpretive question [2]. Second, which policy matters more, fiscal or monetary? Blinder notes that the old dispute over which policy was "powerless" is essentially over: nearly all Keynesians and monetarists now believe that both fiscal and monetary policies affect aggregate demand [2]. Third, is the Phillips curve stable? Phelps and Friedman warned that the public would learn to expect inflation and the trade-off would shift; the stagflation of the 1970s undermined the assumption of fixed parameters [3]. Fourth, do rational expectations make systematic demand management ineffective? New classical macroeconomics held that anticipated changes in the money supply do not affect real output, and new Keynesians replied with sticky prices [2]. Fifth, what caused stagflation? Oil shocks, fiscal imbalances, monetary expansion and real-time data errors may all have contributed [3].

Legacy

Demand management and central banks' mandates carry marks of the Keynesian era [3]. After the 1970s, policy gave more weight to rules, expectations and policy credibility; today most central banks have adopted specific numerical inflation objectives [3]. Keynes's emphasis on uncertainty and effective demand returned in crisis policy.

Hindsight

Equating "Keynesianism" with Keynes's own views ignores his 1923 criticism of the old parity and his position on managed money [1]. Crediting postwar growth entirely to demand management ignores technology and trade. Saying stagflation proved that Keynesian economics had failed ignores the later reconstruction of new Keynesian models.

Aggregate demand, the multiplier, the fiscal-versus-monetary policy debates, monetarism, rational expectations and New Keynesian economics — these later readings of Keynes — follow the Concise Encyclopedia of Economics entry on Econlib [2].

Knowledge-point review

John Maynard Keynes

Definition: John Maynard Keynes was a 20th-century British economist whose General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936) stressed effective demand.

Mechanism: Keynes argued that wage-price rigidity, uncertainty and liquidity preference can leave the economy at underemployment equilibrium, supporting fiscal and monetary intervention.

Example: For example, in the Depression deficient demand and high unemployment meant rate cuts might not spur investment, while spending could fill the gap.

Boundary: Keynes was not simply pro-big-government; he studied expectations, uncertainty and institutions, policy has lags, crowding out and inflation risk, and not all deficits are Keynesian.

postwar neoclassical synthesis

Definition: The postwar neoclassical synthesis combined Keynesian macro with Walrasian general equilibrium and marginal analysis, represented by Samuelson and Hicks.

Mechanism: Hicks's IS-LM joined goods and money markets, Samuelson's textbook spread the mixed economy, and demand management coexisted with market allocation.

Example: For example, IS-LM analyses short-run fiscal and monetary effects through interest rates and output.

Boundary: The synthesis was not original Keynes; IS-LM simplified uncertainty and expectations, ignored supply and institutions, and stagflation later broke pure demand management.

monetarism

Definition: Monetarism, associated with Milton Friedman, stresses money supply, rules and long-run policy neutrality against discretionary fine-tuning.

Mechanism: The quantity theory, natural unemployment and expectation adjustment are central; excess money growth causes inflation and long-run unemployment is structural.

Example: For example, with 1970s inflation, monetarists urged money-growth control rather than demand fine-tuning.

Boundary: Monetarism is not simple printing; money definitions, lags and innovation complicate control, the natural rate depends on institutions and policy, and fiscal and supply shocks matter.

new classical macroeconomics

Definition: New classical macroeconomics stresses rational expectations, market clearing and microfoundations, associated with Lucas, Sargent and Barro.

Mechanism: Rational expectations make systematic policy ineffective, the Lucas critique notes parameters shift with policy, and real business cycle theory attributes fluctuations to technology shocks.

Example: For example, if the public expects easing, wages and prices adjust immediately and short-run stimulus fades.

Boundary: New classical is not a frictionless world; rational expectations are strong, information and sticky prices are ignored, and RBC underplays money and finance.

new Keynesian economics

Definition: New Keynesian economics explains sticky prices and wages, imperfect competition and persistent demand shocks on microfoundations.

Mechanism: Menu costs, staggered pricing, efficiency wages and credit frictions give nominal shocks real effects; policy can stabilise but needs rules and credibility.

Example: For example, menu costs mean a nominal demand fall cuts output and jobs rather than instant price cuts.

Boundary: New Keynesian is not old Keynes; models keep rational expectations and optimisation, sticky sources and financial frictions need specificity, and crisis nonlinearity exceeds models.

post-Keynesian economics and policy debates

Definition: Post-Keynesian economics stresses uncertainty, effective demand, financial instability and history, associated with Joan Robinson and Minsky.

Mechanism: Minsky's financial instability hypothesis sees booms endogenously building leverage and fragility; post-Keynesians reject simple natural rates and representative agents.

Example: For example, a long boom encourages leverage and risk-taking, eventually creating fragility and crisis.

Boundary: Post-Keynesian is not one school; models, policy and money views differ, uncertainty is hard to formalise, and policy debates are often reduced to labels.

Data and institutional check: effective demand

Before using "effective demand" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns multiplier.

Third, the institutional boundary. Phillips curve is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: effective demand

Learning "effective demand" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "multiplier": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "Phillips curve" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of effective demand still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind multiplier reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "effective demand" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "multiplier" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Review questions: effective demand

  1. State the numerator, denominator and unit of "effective demand" in one sentence, then explain why it is not the same metric as "multiplier".
  2. Give an example where a change in "Phillips curve" reverses the conclusion, and name the rule that changed.
  3. Find a time series and show how seasonal adjustment, base period or revision changes its direction.
  4. Design a transfer test: from firm to household, or bank to fund; which parameters must be re-estimated?
  5. Write a falsifiable claim: which observable signal would make you abandon this article's conclusion?

Short answers are enough, but each must leave a number, date or source location. If you cannot answer, the module is still at the noun level; if you can, you have the minimum condition for transferring the concept to new data.

Exercises

  1. What does "deficient effective demand" mean? Why could an economy settle below full employment?
  2. Why did the Phillips curve break down in the 1970s? What roles did expectations and supply shocks play?
  3. "The Employment Act of 1946 proved that Keynesian theory was correct." Which problems of evidence does this sentence ignore?
Suggested answer points
  1. Effective demand is the aggregate demand firms expect to realise. If consumption is constrained by income and investment by expectations, spending may fail to absorb capacity; if wages and prices adjust slowly, the economy can remain at low employment.
  2. If workers and firms expect inflation, nominal wages rise and the trade-off shifts; oil shocks push up costs and unemployment at the same time. A fixed-menu curve ignores expectations and institutions.
  3. The Act established a responsibility and a coordination mechanism, not a test of a theory; its effects depend on implementation, external shocks and measurement. Passing a law cannot confirm all predictions of a model.
  4. Concept check. Explain "effective demand" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
  5. Mechanism chain. Write the transmission from "effective demand" to "multiplier" in three steps and name the step most dependent on an unstated assumption.
