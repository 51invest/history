China's Modern Economic Transition

From the five treaty ports of 1842 to the renminbi of 1948, this article examines how treaty tariffs, modern industry and currency unification reshaped China's economy, and where the statistics and interpretations end.

Problem and scope

This article covers trade, tariffs, industry and money in China from 1842 to 1949. It does not narrate the full political history, and it does not equate "modernisation" with economic growth.

Background

That China was predominantly agricultural, used silver and copper cash, confined foreign trade to Guangzhou, lived on land tax and had no unified central bank is this article's own general framing; none of the three pages cited here states it. What the central-bank article does state is that the mixed silver-based currency system had to be reformed to suit commodity trade, and that the state lacked instruments of financial control and urgently needed a modern system for managing currency[2].

Primary materials

The public pages actually used here are the archives entry on the Treaty of Nanjing and two People's Bank of China articles. Customs trade statistics, local gazetteers and chamber-of-commerce archives are listed only as categories of material, and the claim that customs data cover declared trade alone is this article's judgement, not a statement of the pages cited.

Change

The 1842 Treaty of Nanjing opened Guangzhou, Xiamen, Fuzhou, Ningbo and Shanghai as treaty ports, set an indemnity totalling 21 million silver dollars, and provided that duties on British imports and exports be "fairly fixed by stated regulations"; the archives page reads this as China losing the power to set its own rates and as the beginning of treaty tariffs. The 1843 Charter for the Five Ports of Trade and the Supplementary Treaty of the Bogue (Humen) were imposed as supplements, one clause of which states that British nationals accused of crimes in China are not subject to Chinese law[1].

From the late nineteenth century, government and government-supervised enterprises appeared in armaments, shipping, textiles and mining, but capital, technology and markets were limited, and the ports pulled ahead of the interior. This paragraph is the article's own general framing and is not addressed by any of the three pages cited.

On 3 November 1935 H. H. Kung declared the fabi reform: silver payments stopped, silver nationalised, the notes of the Central Bank of China, the Bank of China and the Bank of Communications made legal tender, and those three banks authorised to buy and sell foreign exchange without limit. The government concentrated silver held by the public to build issue reserves, and by controlling financial resources made material preparation for the coming full-scale war. The "irredeemable" note policy and the "unlimited" foreign-exchange policy, however, planted the seeds of inflation[2].

The governments of North China, Shandong and the Shaanxi-Gansu-Ningxia and Suiyuan border regions agreed to merge the North China Bank, the Beihai Bank and the North-West Peasant Bank, and "on 1 December 1948 the People's Bank of China was founded in Shijiazhuang, Hebei", a unified renminbi being issued at the same time. Local currencies already circulating in the liberated areas were withdrawn in turn and recalled at prescribed ratios, which the branch page lists as 1:100, 1:1000 and 1:2000, and remained in circulation at those ratios until fully recalled; "the renminbi issued from the start until it ceased to circulate on 10 May 1955 forms the first series"[3].

Debates

First, was the change "impact-response" or internally driven? Treaty ports were an external shock, but Ming-Qing market change also mattered. Second, can port growth stand for the whole country? The interior faced transport and institutional constraints, so port data overstate the aggregate. Third, did treaty tariffs strangle domestic industry? Missing protection was one factor, but capital, technology and political disorder mattered as much. Fourth, how should the 1935 reform be judged? The pages support its concentrating silver, building issue reserves and preparing for the war, at the cost of inflation. These four framings are the article's own; only the evidence named under the fourth comes from a cited page.

Legacy

The branch page speaks only of the currency unification itself: issuing a unified renminbi eliminated the currencies of the Nationalist government and ended decades of inflation and a century of foreign notes and gold and silver circulating and being traded in China, and played an important part in economic recovery in the early years of the People's Republic. Treating customs statistics, tariff administration and the central bank together as institutional starting points after 1949, and calling the industrial base thin and regional gaps persistent, are this article's judgements; the cited pages do not say so.

Hindsight

Neither "total failure" nor a "golden decade" fits. Outcomes varied by region, sector and year; national averages hide the contrast between ports and the interior.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "HIS-014". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "treaty ports". Column two states the transmission channel of "treaty tariffs". Column three states the institutional boundary of "modern industry". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "treaty tariffs" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. modern industry is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Exercises

  1. How does a treaty tariff differ from an autonomous tariff, and what might that mean for early industry?
  2. Why can customs trade statistics not represent all trade?
  3. What were the gains and risks of the 1935 fabi reform?
Suggested answer points
  1. A treaty tariff fixes rates by agreement, while an autonomous tariff is set by domestic legislation. The former limits protective duties, but industrial development also depends on capital, technology and markets.
  2. Customs data cover declared trade only; smuggling, native customs and rural markets are excluded, and double counting across ports is possible.
  3. Gains were the concentration of silver, the building of issue reserves and material preparation for the war against Japan; the risk was the seeds of inflation planted by an "irredeemable" note policy and "unlimited" foreign-exchange conversion (all as stated by the cited central-bank article).

  2. Write "treaty ports" and "treaty tariffs" as observable variables and name the time gap between them.
  3. Find one rule change in "modern industry" and explain how it changes sample coverage.
  4. Run a counterfactual with this article's method: change one variable and hold the rest fixed.
  5. If you switched to an independent source, which order of magnitude would move most, and why?
