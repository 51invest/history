The Great Divergence and Institutional Comparison: Evidence from Regional History

Why did Western Europe industrialise first in the nineteenth century while other regions did not? The Great Divergence debate puts geography, institutions, colonisation, energy and knowledge networks on one comparative table. This article adds representative experiences from Europe and the Americas, Asia, Africa and the Middle East, then sets out four checks for institutional comparison.

Problem and scope

This article examines income divergence across Eurasia and the Americas from 1500 to 2000. The point is not a single-cause answer to “why the West led”, but a comparison of four explanations: geography and resources, institutions and property rights, colonisation and coerced labour, and energy and knowledge networks. The data rely mainly on Maddison Project 2023; the institutional comparison uses AJR’s colonial-origins study[1].

Background

Around 1800, income gaps between Western Europe and East Asia were not yet enormous; after nineteenth-century industrialisation they widened quickly. Railways, steam, fossil energy, imperial trade and modern state fiscal capacity interacted. The Great Divergence was not a smooth curve; it was woven from war, disease, trade conditions and institutional change[3].

Primary materials and editions

Maddison Project Database 2023 provides long-run GDP per capita and population estimates; AJR 2001 uses colonial institutional differences to explain long-run development; OECD’s How Was Life? Volume II provides well-being and inequality comparisons since 1820. Their measures differ: GDP, institutional quality and multidimensional well-being are not interchangeable, so the article labels the measure[2].

Change and timeline

From 1500 to 1800, major Eurasian regions experienced agricultural growth, trade expansion and fiscal competition, but income gaps remained limited. In the nineteenth century, Britain first combined coal, iron, steam and factory organisation, raising productivity; other regions were colonised or remained sovereign but reformed slowly. In the twentieth century, Latin America, East Asia and Africa followed different paths, showing that divergence was not one event but a process of continued divergence and catch-up.

Regional experiences and institutional comparison

Regional comparison is not a set of separate success stories, one per continent. It asks the same questions of every case: what were the initial conditions, what was the factor endowment, who enforced the institutions, how did external shocks transmit, and with what measure was the outcome recorded. The next four sections apply that frame to Europe and the Americas, Asia, Africa and the Middle East, and finally set out the checks for putting all of them on one table[1][2][3].

Europe and the Americas: early growth, colonisation and institutional divergence

Europe and the Americas are not single units. Europe contains the early industrial leaders, Britain and Belgium, as well as later catch-up cases such as Germany, Sweden, and the southern and eastern peripheries. The Americas must be split between settler economies such as the United States and Canada and the plantation, mineral and debt economies of Latin America and the Caribbean. The comparison is about the conditions of leaders and latecomers, not a claim that the Western European path is a universal template[1][7].

The mechanism in Europe combined interstate competition, fiscal capacity, property rights and contract enforcement, coal and steam, colonial trade and rising literacy. Britain in the 18th–19th centuries joined coal, iron, the steam engine and factory organisation; Germany and the United States caught up in the 19th century with railways, education, banks and tariff protection. In the Americas, land, labour and colonial institutions shaped the path more strongly: the United States and Canada had wider settler property rights and immigration, while many Latin American regions developed large estates, coerced labour and highly unequal power structures, and remained exposed to primary exports and external capital after independence[1][6][7].

A teaching contrast can hold the starting income constant and vary the institutions: group A has enforceable property rights and broad provision of schools and roads; group B has resource rents and a narrow elite coalition. Given the same external price, investment and human capital diffuse more easily in A, while B may use rents to suppress competition. The contrast is not a historical statistic; it shows how institutions act through investment, education and the distribution of opportunity.

The boundary is that Europe did not follow one road, and Western Europe’s early lead does not imply that southern or eastern Europe failed. The United States and Canada cannot represent the whole Americas. The direction and strength of the colonial legacy depended on the coloniser, local population density, resource conditions and the post-independence political coalition; a single “good or bad institutions” label cannot explain it. Comparisons must name the country, period, variable and measure, or “why Europe led” becomes a hindsight story.

Asia: agrarian states, trade networks and compressed industrialisation

Asia is not captured by a single “East Asian miracle”. Japan, Korea, Taiwan (China), Hong Kong, Singapore, Southeast Asia, mainland China, India and South Asia started from different conditions of state capacity, land institutions and external environment. The unit of comparison can be an economy, a city, a region or a group; treating “Asia” as a culturally homogeneous whole erases those differences[1][4].

At least three combinations appear. First, the agrarian base and land institutions: Japan, Korea and Taiwan (China) carried out land reform after the Second World War, and smallholder farming plus basic education supplied labour and markets for industry. Second, state capacity and export orientation: Japan, Korea and Singapore used industrial policy, credit, education, infrastructure and export discipline to drive compressed industrialisation; mainland China changed its growth path after 1978 through rural reform, opening and coastal processing zones. Third, trade networks and services: Hong Kong and Singapore relied on ports, finance and re-export trade; India expanded services and information-technology exports after the 1990s. The three combinations share high investment, human capital and external linkages, but their sequence and institutions differ[4].

A teaching timeline can place Japan’s Meiji industrialisation after 1868, Korea’s export orientation after the 1960s, and mainland China’s reform and opening after 1978 on one axis: the starting points were an agrarian society, a low-income post-war economy and a planned economy; the external environments were imperial competition, Cold War aid and globalisation. All three are called “industrialisation”, but the division of labour among the state, land, foreign capital and markets was not the same. The contrast trains students to see one mechanism in different combinations, not to rank countries.

The boundary is that the East Asian “developmental state” is not a universal template. State direction can produce rents, corruption and misallocation; the 1997 Asian financial crisis exposed fragile financial regulation and external debt. City economies cannot transfer their experience directly to large agrarian countries; mainland China’s scale, India’s demography and South Asia’s federal structures impose different constraints. Explaining growth through “Asian values” merges institutions, geography and policy choice into cultural essence; this article does not use that explanation.

Africa and the Middle East: colonial borders, resource rents and state capacity

Africa and the Middle East are no less diverse than other regions. Africa includes resource exporters, agricultural exporters, island and small open economies; the Middle East and North Africa include high-income oil exporters, oil importers and conflict-affected economies. The comparison is not a search for a common “failure” but an examination of how colonial borders, resource rents, state capacity and external intervention combine[3][5][6].

Colonial borders in Africa put different societies, languages and trade networks inside a single administration; after independence, state-building started from a low tax base, thin infrastructure and primary-export dependence. Resource rents can support public spending, but they can also weaken the tax contract, crowd out manufacturing and amplify fiscal cycles; agricultural exports and tourism depend more on infrastructure, land rights and external markets. Oil rents in the Middle East can finance a large public sector, subsidies and public services, but oil-price swings, youth employment, debt and conflict turn that model into a source of vulnerability. The shared mechanism is “rents–state–external shocks”, not a fixed cultural trait[3][5][6].

A teaching comparison can set Nigeria’s oil revenue and fiscal dependence beside Ghana’s cocoa and export structure, Kenya’s horticulture and tourism, the Gulf states’ oil-financed budgets and Egypt’s import substitution and debt pressure. None is the product of a single cause. The same resource rent can become public investment where institutions are relatively stable and the state has bargaining capacity, as in Botswana, or a site of rent contestation where conflict or elite capture is severe. The contrast separates “resources as a condition” from “resources as destiny”.

The boundary is that the resource curse is not a law: Botswana and Mauritius show that institutions, bargaining and diversification can change the path, but their successes cannot be copied mechanically to every country. The effects of colonial borders, state capacity and conflict intensity need case-specific evidence; African statistical coverage, informal activity and price data have gaps, and conflict data in the Middle East are even harder to compare. “African failure” or “Middle Eastern wealth” as a whole is a label that flattens regional differences.

Institutional and development comparison: four checks

Institutional and development comparison puts regional experiences on the same set of questions: initial conditions, factor endowments, institutional enforcement, external shocks and measurement. It does not rank civilisations. Institutions are not a synonym for “good policy”; they are a bundle of property rights, contracts, taxation, public goods and constraints on violence. What matters is not what the legal text says but who enforces it, against whom, and who bears the cost[2][7].

Institutions affect development through three channels: property rights and contract enforcement shape investment and long projects; taxation and state capacity shape infrastructure, education and redistribution; violence and conflict constraints shape risk and transaction costs. Institutions are also changed by growth: industrialisation creates new interest groups, cities and educational demands, which reshape tax, labour and property arrangements. That feedback means “institutions → growth” is not a one-way arrow[2][7].

A teaching table can apply four checks to every regional case: first, measure—GDP, real wages, height, literacy and life expectancy cannot substitute for one another; second, counterfactual—what would have happened without colonisation, oil or foreign aid; third, mechanism—did institutions work through investment, education, technology diffusion or violence; fourth, sample—are failures included alongside successes. AJR use settler mortality as an instrument for institutions, and Engerman–Sokoloff use factor endowments to explain divergent paths in the Americas; both turn mechanisms into testable propositions, and both carry identification assumptions and scope limits[2][7].

The boundary is that institutional variables can become tautological: fast growth means good institutions, slow growth means bad ones. Instrument exclusion, historical data coverage and the measurement of colonial experience are disputed. Institutional comparison can offer conditional explanations; it cannot promise that copying a set of rules will succeed. Path dependence is not destiny: war, crisis, policy coalitions and technology can change the trajectory, but the direction and speed still need evidence[2][7].

Debates

The first debate is geographic determinism: do climate, disease and resources explain enough? The second is institutional determinism: are property rights, rule of law and constraints on violence the key? The third is colonisation and extraction: did colonisers create the gap through coerced labour and resource extraction? Each debate needs counterfactuals, comparable data and mechanism evidence, not correlation alone.

Legacy and influence

The Great Divergence debate influenced development economics, global history and institutional analysis. It reminds policymakers that institutions are not “copying a legal text” but a bundle of implementation capacity, tax base, property arrangements and constraints on violence. It also reminds researchers that long-run data carry estimation error; one GDP curve is not all of history.

Hindsight and analogy

Calling Western industrialisation an “inevitable victory of institutions” is hindsight bias: winners’ institutional stories tend to omit war, colonisation and resources. Calling institutions “culture genes” also oversimplifies. Using the Great Divergence on contemporary development requires checking technology, global division of labour and state capacity, not lifting a nineteenth-century script.
