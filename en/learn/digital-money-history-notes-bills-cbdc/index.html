Historical Echoes of Digital Money: From Notes and Bills to CBDC

Digital money is not entirely new: notes, bills, bank deposits and credit cards all did the same thing, turning money from an object into a transferable accounting claim. CBDC differs in the issuer being the central bank, the liability being a central-bank liability, and programmability.

Problem and scope

Digital money is a monetary claim held in electronic accounts. It is not entirely new: banknotes are paper transferable claims, bills are transferable credit instruments, deposits are bank accounting liabilities and cards carry payment instructions.

CBDC is a digital liability issued by the central bank, either retail (for the public) or wholesale (for financial institutions). The key difference from commercial-bank money is the issuer: CBDC is a central-bank liability, deposits are commercial-bank liabilities.

Historical echoes run from commercial bills to banknotes, deposits and card payments; monetary evolution has continually solved three problems: trust, settlement speed and cross-border usability. CBDC changes the issuer and technology to the central bank and distributed ledgers[1][2].

Worked example

Suppose M2 is 10 billion yuan: 1 billion cash, 4 billion demand deposits and 5 billion time and savings deposits. If 500 million of retail CBDC is issued and households convert demand deposits, M2 is unchanged but the liability structure shifts: central-bank liabilities rise and commercial-bank liabilities fall.

If CBDC pays no interest, households may hold it only for payments; if it pays interest, it could replace deposits on a large scale and affect bank funding. If programmable, it can target subsidies but raises privacy and surveillance issues.

CBDC does not change the money stock but changes its structure, bank intermediation and payments. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Background

Bills and banknotes already carried cross-border payments in the Mediterranean and early modern Europe; the Bank of England tied government debt, notes and discounting together in 1694; US free banking produced competing note issues in the nineteenth century; cards and electronic transfers digitised payment instructions in the twentieth; M-Pesa brought mobile money to places without branches in 2007; central banks began CBDC research after 2014.

Primary materials and editions

Sources include Bank of England and central-bank archives, note and bill specimens, nineteenth-century US banking laws, card-network rules, M-Pesa transaction data, BIS CBDC surveys and central-bank white papers (the Bahamas' Sand Dollar, China's e-CNY, the Eastern Caribbean's DCash). Separate design objectives from marketing: privacy, limits, interest and offline capacity are choices, not technical necessities.

Change and trade-offs

Trade-off 1: payment efficiency versus privacy. CBDC can cut settlement costs and improve inclusion, but transaction data concentrate at the central bank or intermediaries; privacy needs design.

Trade-off 2: financial stability versus disintermediation. Interest-bearing CBDC can draw deposits in stress and weaken banks; limits, zero interest or tiering are needed.

Trade-off 3: cross-border efficiency versus regulation. Multi-CBDC arrangements can cut cross-border costs but raise capital-flow, exchange-control and AML challenges.

CBDC is not 'digital cash' alone; it redesigns monetary sovereignty, bank structure and payment networks[3][4].

Boundaries and mistakes

Boundary 1: CBDC is not cryptocurrency. CBDC is a central-bank liability, stable and legal tender; crypto is a private asset, volatile and without sovereign backing.

Boundary 2: CBDC is not mobile payment. Alipay and WeChat Pay are payment interfaces for commercial-bank money; CBDC is central-bank money itself.

Boundary 3: technology is not the only variable. Account-based versus token-based design, privacy and law determine usability; distributed ledgers are a tool.

Mistake 1: CBDC will eliminate cash. Most designs keep cash; CBDC complements it.

Mistake 2: CBDC solves all payment problems. It improves domestic settlement and inclusion, but cross-border, privacy and stability need institutional design.

Historiographical debate

Four disputes dominate: account-based versus token-based design; whether to pay interest and impose limits; whether privacy sits with the central bank or intermediaries; and whether cross-border use weakens capital controls. Supporters stress inclusion and settlement efficiency; critics fear disintermediation, surveillance and faster runs. History suggests payment tools succeed through networks, trust and regulatory boundaries, not technology alone.

Legacy and influence: from notes to central-bank digital currency

Paper money did not appear suddenly. Song-era jiaozi, Yuan paper notes, European banknotes and treasury bills were all transferable claims. Their common foundation was trust: whether the issuer could redeem, whether law recognised them and whether networks accepted them.

The banknote era: in the 19th-century US free-banking period, many banks issued notes whose discounts varied with distance and reputation; clearing houses and central banks gradually unified the currency. Central-bank monopoly issuance solved runs, clearing and monetary-policy transmission.

Electronic money: credit cards, debit cards and mobile payments turn bank deposits into transferable payment instructions; behind them remain commercial-bank liabilities and central-bank settlement. Mobile payments changed the experience, not the monetary hierarchy.

Central-bank digital currency (CBDC) is the central bank's liability in digital form, retail or wholesale. Retail CBDC could change disintermediation, privacy and cross-border payments; wholesale CBDC targets settlement and securities. Design choices include interest, limits, anonymity and offline capability.

Stablecoins are private liabilities in digital form, like banknotes but without deposit insurance or a lender of last resort; stability depends on reserves and redemption mechanics.

Historical lesson: money's form changes, but the hierarchy (central bank-bank-private) and the trust foundation do not disappear; innovation often starts private and is later integrated by the public sector.

Common misuses: treating CBDC as 'digital cash' while ignoring account design; treating stablecoins as 'new money' while ignoring their liability nature; treating digitalisation as 'decentralisation'.

Cases: free banking, mobile money and CBDC pilots

US free banking (1837-1863): state-chartered banks issued their own notes, with discounts varying by reputation and distance; clearing houses and the 1863 National Banking Act gradually unified them. The lesson: dispersed issuance lowers trust and raises clearing costs.

Kenya's M-Pesa: a mobile network operator provided payments to the unbanked; success came from agent networks, regulatory tolerance and phone penetration, not 'monetary innovation' alone.

China's mobile payments: Alipay and WeChat Pay linked bank accounts to payment instructions and changed retail experience; the DC/EP pilot digitalises central-bank liabilities with controllable anonymity and dual offline capability.

Sweden's e-krona: as cash use falls, the central bank explores a retail CBDC, focusing on offline use, privacy and the commercial-bank role.

Comparison: private innovation solves use cases first, public authorities then solve clearing, regulation and monetary sovereignty; CBDC success depends on use cases, privacy and bank-system cooperation, not technology alone.

Hindsight and analogy

Calling CBDC unprecedented is hindsight bias. Bills, banknotes, deposits and mobile money all create transferable accounting claims; failures such as DigiCash show that privacy and merchant networks matter. Directly likening CBDC to banknotes misses three new features: central-bank liability, programmability and concentrated data. Historical echoes raise questions; they do not settle outcomes.

Monetary-history checklist

When comparing bills, banknotes, deposits, mobile money and CBDC, ask five questions:

  1. Who issues it and what liability is it? Commercial notes, central-bank notes, deposits and CBDC rest on different credit; who backstops them in a crisis defines the trust boundary.
  2. Where is payment finality? Bilateral bookkeeping, net settlement or real-time gross settlement; stronger finality demands more liquidity.
  3. Who protects privacy? Transaction data sits with banks, platforms or the central bank; visibility and traceability shape surveillance and exclusion risk.
  4. How does the network start? Merchant, bank and user adoption is decisive; many technically advanced tools failed on a small network.
  5. Who loses when it fails? Freezes, hacks, de-pegging and insolvency rules reveal institutional quality better than white-paper daily flows.

Separate issuance, settlement, privacy, network and failure handling; only then can you tell whether a new money is an old function with new tech or a real shift in power.

What would change the conclusion

The historical analogy depends on three new features:

  1. Privacy technology improves. If zero-knowledge proofs and offline payments satisfy privacy and compliance together, surveillance concerns around CBDC fall.
  2. Disintermediation is contained. If limits, zero interest and tiered wallets curb deposit flight, the impact on banks is smaller than feared.
  3. Cross-border coordination fails. If national CBDC standards are incompatible, cross-border efficiency gains are limited and the network effect in the historical analogy does not form.
  4. Programmability is restricted. If law and politics prohibit targeted programming, CBDC is closer to digital cash than a policy tool.

Chapter takeaways

  • Paper notes, banknotes, e-money and CBDC are claims at different layers.
  • Money's form changes, but the central bank-bank-private hierarchy and trust foundation do not disappear.
  • Free banking's lesson: dispersed issuance lowers trust and raises clearing costs.
  • Stablecoins are private liabilities without deposit insurance or a lender of last resort.
  • CBDC success depends on use cases, privacy and bank-system cooperation, not technology alone.
