A History of Funds, Indices and Investment Thought

From the first U.S. open-end mutual fund in 1924 to the first index fund for individual investors in 1976, how indices, funds and investment ideas reinforced one another; the disputed start year of the price averages could not be checked against a source and is kept only as this article's note.

Problem and scope

This article examines U.S. indices, mutual funds and investment thought from the late nineteenth century to 1976, focusing on the shift from price records to owning the market. It does not evaluate fund performance or cover ETFs, factor investing or the global fund industry.

Background

Investors need comparable market measures and diversified vehicles. Nineteenth-century quotation sheets and speculation manuals supplied rules of thumb; twentieth-century company research, long-run return statistics and portfolio theory turned "which stock?" into "whether to own the market."

Primary materials

The MFS and Vanguard company histories are the materials actually retrieved in full. Both present their own product as the "first", so they are interested accounts; this article registers only the sentences those pages state verbatim, and prospectuses and index methodology documents remain unchecked. Jeremy Siegel's "Stock Market" entry for Econlib could not be retrieved here and has been removed from the source list; the dates and long-run return conclusion formerly credited to it are no longer cited claims.

Change

References differ on whether the Dow Jones averages begin in 1896 or 1897, the S&P 500 was launched in 1957, and Nasdaq grew out of the over-the-counter market around 1970. These three dates were formerly credited to Siegel's Econlib entry, which could not be retrieved, so the sentences were never checked against it; they stand only as this article's own timeline markers and a noted discrepancy. Indices turned dispersed quotations into comparable series, but their constituents and weighting determine the answer.

MFS's official history states: "That commitment started in 1924, when MFS launched the first US open-end mutual fund to give millions of everyday investors access to the markets" [1]. Vanguard's history states that "Bogle launched Vanguard 500 Index Fund on August 31, 1976, as First Index Investment Trust" and that "The first index fund for individual investors embodied the Vanguard founder’s belief that most investors are better served by owning the whole stock market, at low cost, rather than trying to beat it". On the launch: "The offering raised a little more than $11 million", which Bogle later called "an abject failure", while the product began as an experiment that was, to some, "Bogle’s Folly" [2]. The argument that long-run stock returns exceed bond returns came from the entry that could not be retrieved, so this article no longer attributes it.

Debates

First, active versus passive: index funds assume prices already reflect information, while active managers claim research can add value; fees, taxes and survivorship bias complicate comparison. Second, index construction: the Dow Jones averages and the S&P 500 differ in constituents and weighting, so the same market can yield different answers. Third, fund governance: mutual funds bring diversification to individuals but also fees, sales incentives and agency problems.

Legacy

Indices became benchmarks and asset-allocation tools, and low-cost passive products changed the fund industry; but a "market portfolio" still depends on the investable universe, free float and rebalancing rules.

Hindsight

The 1976 fund's disappointing fundraising does not prove indexing was destined to succeed; today's scale does not prove it was an obvious choice then.

Knowledge-point review

valuation history

Definition: Valuation evolved from discounting future income, asset value and comparison to DCF, residual income and multiples.

Mechanism: Fisher stressed present value of future income, Graham and Dodd stressed margin of safety and assets, and modern DCF structures growth, risk and capital cost.

Example: For example, Graham looked for cheap stocks via net current assets and earnings stability, while DCF discounts free cash flow.

Boundary: Valuation is not one formula; assumptions, accounting and information change results, historical methods have limits, and model precision is not market truth.

value and growth investing

Definition: Value investing buys relatively cheap, cash-generative assets, while growth investing buys fast-growing assets with high current valuations.

Mechanism: Value relies on mean reversion, margin of safety and dividends; growth on reinvestment returns and long-term cash flow; rates, inflation and sentiment drive rotation.

Example: For example, low-P/E value stocks may benefit from rising rates, while high-growth tech wins in low-rate optimism.

Boundary: Value is not just cheap and growth is not just fast; value traps, growth bubbles and definition changes break styles, and the two often overlap.

portfolio theory

Definition: Modern portfolio theory builds diversified portfolios from expected returns, variance and correlation, associated with Markowitz, Sharpe and Fama.

Mechanism: Markowitz's 1952 efficient frontier, Sharpe's CAPM and risk adjustment expanded risk from single assets to portfolios.

Example: For example, low-correlation assets can cut volatility at the same return.

Boundary: Portfolio theory is not universal; input errors, tails, liquidity and behaviour hurt out-of-sample, CAPM's single beta is criticised, and diversification cannot remove systemic risk.

index investing history

Definition: Index investing replicates the market portfolio, rooted in evidence that active managers struggle and demand for low cost.

Mechanism: Index funds, ETFs and pension allocation drove passivisation; cap weighting, rules and fees determine tracking and governance.

Example: For example, the first US index fund appeared in the 1970s, followed by ETFs and global allocation.

Boundary: Indexing is not passively harmless; concentration, cap-weighted bubbles, voting and governance influence are amplified by passive money, and rules can be gamed.

behavioural finance history

Definition: Behavioural finance studies how cognitive bias, emotion and social factors affect prices and decisions, associated with Kahneman, Tversky and Thaler.

Mechanism: Prospect theory, loss aversion, overconfidence and limited attention challenge efficient markets and can create mispricing and bubbles.

Example: For example, investors are overly sensitive to losses, selling winners early and holding losers.

Boundary: Behavioural finance is not simply “markets are irrational”; biases can be ecologically rational, limits to arbitrage sustain mispricing, and interventions can be manipulated, so institutions still matter.

quantitative and institutional investing

Definition: Quantitative investing uses data and models systematically, and institutional investing evolved through pensions, mutual funds, hedge funds, ETFs and algorithmic trading.

Mechanism: Quant expanded from statistical arbitrage and factor models to machine learning; institutionalisation changed market structure, liquidity and governance, with passive-active competition.

Example: For example, statistical arbitrage grew in the 1980s, factor funds in the 1990s, and ETFs and algo trading spread in the 2000s.

Boundary: Quant is not steady profit; overfitting, crowding, flash crashes and model risk cause losses, and institutionalisation can concentrate power, homogenise and worsen liquidity crises.

Exercises

  1. Why is an index both a "market" and a "set of construction rules"?
  2. What did the 1924 mutual fund and the 1976 index fund each solve?
  3. What are the limits of using a fund company's history to prove a "first"?
Suggested answer points
  1. An index represents a market through chosen constituents and weights; different constituents, weights and rebalancing rules produce different results.
  2. The mutual fund gave small investors diversification and professional management; the index fund gave them a low-cost portfolio close to the market.
  3. Company histories emphasise their own priority and contribution; prospectuses, regulatory filings and third-party data are needed to check dates and definitions.
