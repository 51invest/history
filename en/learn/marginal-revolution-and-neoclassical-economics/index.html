The Marginal Revolution and Neoclassical Economics

From the 1870s, Jevons, Menger and Walras shifted value theory toward marginal utility, and Marshall combined utility, cost and time; this article sets out the claims, evidence and limits of that change.

Problem and scope

In the 1870s, value theory moved from labour quantities and cost of production toward the utility of the last unit. This article uses British, Austrian and Swiss works from 1871 to 1920 to explain marginal analysis and Marshall's synthesis. It does not cover all of microeconomics, and it does not treat "neoclassical" as a label the authors used for themselves.

Background

Classical political economy explained exchange value by labour or cost, and the water-diamond paradox is on the record inside that tradition: Smith's Wealth of Nations, Book I ch. iv, says that things with the greatest value in use often have little or no value in exchange, that water is the most useful and yet purchases scarce any thing [3]. “Had trouble with” here means the labour theory of value left the paradox unresolved, not that Smith never stated it. Industrialisation widened markets, economics became a university profession, and mathematics and physics supplied new tools. The German historical school criticised abstract deduction.

Primary texts

Jevons's Theory of Political Economy (1871) explains value by the "final degree of utility": the utility of an additional unit falls as the stock rises, and the exchange ratio depends on both parties' valuation of the last unit. He named his own "law of indifference": in the same open market, at any one moment, there cannot be two prices for the same kind of article [4]. The volume's closing bibliography also lists Menger's 1871 Vienna volume alongside Walras as independent discoveries [1][4].

Menger's Grundsätze der Volkswirtschaftslehre appeared in Vienna at Wilhelm Braumüller in 1871. Its third chapter treats the essence and origin of goods-value and, in a separate section, the laws regulating the value of goods of a higher order [6]. Calling the book subjective and non-mathematical is this article's judgement of its textual form, not Menger's self-description. Walras's Elements of Pure Economics (1874–1877) describes market interdependence with simultaneous equations; the date and the "mathematical treatment" characterisation rest on Ingram's 1915 history [7], and the original has not been consulted.

Marshall's Principles of Economics (1890) let marginal utility and cost of production jointly determine normal price. His own sentence is that disputing whether value is governed by utility or by cost of production is like disputing which blade of a pair of scissors cuts the paper [5]. He distinguished a very short, a short and a long period — the eighth edition uses no term "market period" — and defined elasticity of demand and consumer's surplus [2][5].

Main debates

First, is value determined by utility or by cost? Jevons still let cost affect supply, and Marshall let both determine price; the claim that utility simply replaced cost is too strong. Second, was the change a rupture or a gradual shift? The three books appeared independently in 1871 — the 1915 history dates Menger's first book to that year, and the edition page for the original calls it one of the works that appeared independently in 1871 [6][7] — yet classical questions of cost and distribution did not disappear. Third, is mathematics necessary? Menger rejected mathematical form, while Walras and Marshall used it; formalisation improved consistency but could compress institutions and history. Fourth, can marginal productivity explain distribution? Clark made “the law of the final productivity of labor and of capital” his law of distribution [8], and the aggregation difficulty is posed by Clark himself, who asks of “the productive power of the final unit of social capital” what such a final unit is “and in what sense can it be called social?” [8]. Wicksteed writes the marginal quantities into the theory of exchange: as one stock grows, “the marginal usefulness of U increases and that of X diminishes” [9]. The later criticism of Clark's capital theory and the dimension of power are kept as this article's judgement; no verbatim source for them was read.

Legacy

Marginal analysis became the basic language of economics: opportunity cost, marginal revenue, elasticity, consumer surplus and general equilibrium all develop from it. In this article “neoclassical” labels a set of assumptions about rational choice, maximisation and equilibrium, and that definition is the article's own usage note; the pointer registered as source 3 (Weintraub's entry) is still unread in this environment, so it carries no sentence here. Institutionally Marshall's own footnote records that the section is “reproduced from a Plea for the creation of a curriculum in economics” addressed to the University of Cambridge in 1902 “and conceded in the following year” [5]; that the Cambridge school held a leading position in the early twentieth century is this article's judgement, and it left questions that Keynes would attack.

Hindsight

Treating diminishing marginal utility as a universal psychological law turns an analytical tool into an empirical claim. Treating “neoclassical” as a single block ignores Menger's non-mathematical tradition, and it also ignores Marshall's own insistence on induction: in the Principles he writes that such principles “need to be supplemented by specific experience, and applied in harmony with, and often in subordination to, a ceaseless study of new facts, a ceaseless search for new inductions” [5]. Reading 1871 through today's textbook can insert the later term "marginal utility" into Jevons's "final degree of utility".

Knowledge-point review

William Stanley Jevons

Definition: William Stanley Jevons was a 19th-century British economist, a founder of marginal utility theory who also studied coal, money and business cycles.

Mechanism: Jevons explained exchange value by marginal utility, diminishing utility and exchange ratios; The Coal Question discussed resource depletion and Money and the Mechanism of Exchange analysed money.

Example: For example, water is vital but has low marginal utility, while diamonds have little use but high marginal utility, explaining price differences.

Boundary: Jevons was not only about utility; his resource pessimism was partly overtaken by technology and new reserves, and marginal utility alone cannot explain prices because institutions, costs and income matter.

Carl Menger

Definition: Carl Menger was an Austrian economist and a leader of the marginal utility revolution, emphasising subjective value, individualism and spontaneous order.

Mechanism: Menger started from human needs and the causal structure of goods, distinguished lower and higher-order goods, derived value from marginal utility and opposed the German historical school's method.

Example: For example, bread directly satisfies need as a lower-order good, while flour and ovens are higher-order goods whose value derives from final consumption.

Boundary: Menger was not a mathematical general-equilibrium theorist; subjective value and institutional evolution are hard to test, do not explain all prices, and market power and money still matter.

Léon Walras

Definition: Léon Walras was a 19th-century French economist who founded general equilibrium theory, describing conditions for all markets to clear simultaneously.

Mechanism: Walras used excess demand, price adjustment and tâtonnement to explore equilibrium, linked marginal utility to scarcity and discussed money and existence.

Example: For example, many prices interact, and general equilibrium requires simultaneous adjustment across markets.

Boundary: Walras was not a perfect model; existence, stability and the auctioneer assumption are criticised, and real frictions, information and power limit direct application.

Alfred Marshall and equilibrium-welfare theory

Definition: Alfred Marshall was a 19th-20th century British economist who synthesised marginal utility and supply-demand, built partial equilibrium analysis, and welfare economics developed through Pigou.

Mechanism: Marshall used supply and demand curves, elasticity, consumer surplus and short versus long run; Pigou discussed externalities and social welfare.

Example: For example, Marshall analyses equilibrium price and quantity in one market, and consumer surplus measures willingness to pay above price.

Boundary: Marshall was not just curves; partial equilibrium ignores feedback and macro, welfare theory faces externalities, distribution and value judgments, and perfect competition often fails.

Data and institutional check: marginal utility

Before using "marginal utility" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns general equilibrium.

Third, the institutional boundary. elasticity of demand is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: marginal utility

Learning "marginal utility" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "general equilibrium": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "elasticity of demand" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of marginal utility still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind general equilibrium reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "marginal utility" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "general equilibrium" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Review questions: marginal utility

  1. State the numerator, denominator and unit of "marginal utility" in one sentence, then explain why it is not the same metric as "general equilibrium".
  2. Give an example where a change in "elasticity of demand" reverses the conclusion, and name the rule that changed.
  3. Find a time series and show how seasonal adjustment, base period or revision changes its direction.
  4. Design a transfer test: from firm to household, or bank to fund; which parameters must be re-estimated?
  5. Write a falsifiable claim: which observable signal would make you abandon this article's conclusion?

Short answers are enough, but each must leave a number, date or source location. If you cannot answer, the module is still at the noun level; if you can, you have the minimum condition for transferring the concept to new data.

Exercises

  1. Use total and marginal utility to explain the price difference between water and diamonds. What does the explanation not prove?
  2. Why does Marshall compare supply and demand to the two blades of a pair of scissors? What does the metaphor leave out?
  3. "The marginal revolution replaced cost with utility, so cost no longer matters." What is wrong with this sentence?
Suggested answer points
  1. Water has high total utility, but its abundant stock makes the last unit's utility low; diamonds are the reverse. This links price to scarcity but does not prove utility is measurable or preferences stable.
  2. Price is determined jointly by supply and demand; one blade alone does not cut paper. The metaphor omits adjustment time, market structure, information and institutions.
  3. Jevons still let cost affect supply, and Marshall put cost and utility into one equilibrium. The revolution changed the centre of value theory, not the relevance of cost.
  1. Concept check. Explain "marginal utility" in no more than three sentences and give a small case where it does not apply.
  2. Mechanism chain. Write the transmission from "marginal utility" to "general equilibrium" in three steps and name the step most dependent on an unstated assumption.
