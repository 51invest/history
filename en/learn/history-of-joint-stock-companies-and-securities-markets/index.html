A History of Joint-Stock Companies and Securities Markets

From the Bank of England's charter in 1694 and the South Sea Bubble to the 1792 Buttonwood Agreement and the 1929 crash, how companies, exchanges and regulation shaped one another.

Problem and scope

This article examines how joint-stock companies and securities markets shaped each other in Britain and the United States from the late seventeenth century to the 1930s. It focuses on capital raising, trading rules and regulation after crises. It does not cover modern issuance review, market making or a global comparison of exchanges.

Background

Long-distance trade, war finance and railways required capital that was long-term and transferable. Chartered companies combined privileges with shares, and shareholders risked their subscriptions. Once shares could change hands, quotations, commissions, settlement and credit moved beyond personal networks and became public rules.

Primary materials

Charters, brokers' agreements, exchange constitutions and contemporary accounts are the main materials. This article relies on the Bank of England's official history, W. C. Van Antwerp's 1914 book The Stock Exchange from Within and Federal Reserve History. The first two are near-contemporary records of the institutions themselves, one official and one a participant's account; the third supplies a later timeline and statistics.

Change

In 1694 the Bank of England was founded - its history page states verbatim "The Bank of England was founded as a private bank in 1694 to act as banker to the Government." - so the war-financing purpose is not presented here as a statement of that page. In 1720 the South Sea Bubble burst and ruined thousands of investors; the page heads that episode "1720 South Sea Bubble: the first financial crisis in the Bank of England’s history"[1], while the loss of confidence in joint-stock finance remains a synthesis of this article.

In New York, on 17 May 1792, twenty-four brokers signed an agreement under a tree opposite what is now 60 Wall Street to charge a commission of at least 0.25 percent; the pact later became known as the Buttonwood Agreement (Van Antwerp). In 1817 the brokers completed a formal organisation and fixed their meeting place at the Merchants' Exchange (Van Antwerp). Railways and government debt expanded listings in the nineteenth century, and margin trading grew. Van Antwerp's 1914 account says brokers had no uniform margin requirement and set it by the quality of the security, the standing of the buyer and the condition of the market (Van Antwerp)[2].

In September 1929 the Dow Jones Industrial Average (the Dow) reached 381 - the Federal Reserve essay reads verbatim "The Dow Jones Industrial Average increased six-fold from sixty-three in August 1921 to 381 in September 1929."[3]. Panic selling is traditionally dated to 24 October, but that page never gives 24 October or "Black Thursday" (0 word faces), so this article states the peak-to-crash sequence only as the page does: "On Black Monday, October 28, 1929, the Dow declined nearly 13 percent." and "On the following day, Black Tuesday, the market dropped nearly 12 percent.", with "By mid-November, the Dow had lost almost half of its value."[3]. The essay itself is dated "Written as of November 22, 2013."[3].

Debates

First, was the joint-stock company a device for mobilising capital or for transferring risk to the public? The South Sea episode supports the second view, railway finance the first. Second, how should self-regulation and public regulation divide? The 1929 crash is often used to argue for stronger regulation, but leverage, banks and policy all contributed, so the crash cannot be blamed on speculation alone. Third, margin trading supplies liquidity and amplifies falls; its net effect depends on clearing and credit conditions.

Legacy

Chartered companies gave way to general incorporation, exchanges became regulated markets, and central banks took on lender-of-last-resort roles. Disclosure, commissions and margin remain core institutions.

Hindsight

The outcomes of 1720 and 1929 were not knowable in advance. Treating bubbles as inherent in joint-stock companies, or regulation as the only remedy, substitutes hindsight for the constraints of the time.

Knowledge-point review

joint-stock companies

Definition: A joint-stock company divides capital into shares, with shareholders bearing risk and sharing profit, often alongside limited liability and corporate personality.

Mechanism: It pools capital, spreads risk and separates management through share issuance; governance, disclosure and limited liability define shareholder rights.

Example: For example, the Dutch East India Company issued shares in 1602 to pool capital and share risk for long-distance trade.

Boundary: Joint-stock companies are not naturally efficient; agency problems, controlling-shareholder expropriation and bubbles hurt minorities, limited liability can encourage risk-taking, and institutions matter.

exchanges

Definition: An exchange is a marketplace concentrating securities, commodity or derivative trading, providing price discovery, liquidity and clearing.

Mechanism: Exchanges evolved from merchant gatherings and coffee houses into formal markets, with rules, membership, listing and disclosure building trust; regulation followed crises.

Example: For example, Amsterdam and London exchanges provided secondary markets for shares, bonds and commodities.

Boundary: Exchanges are not neutral platforms; information asymmetry, manipulation, runs and regulatory arbitrage cause crises, member interests conflict with public oversight, and formalisation is not fairness.

government bonds

Definition: Government bonds are state-issued debt paying interest and principal, forming sovereign markets and benchmark rates.

Mechanism: The market funds governments, supports central-bank operations and supplies safe assets; maturity, credit and liquidity shape the yield curve.

Example: For example, British gilts and Dutch bonds financed long wars and developed active secondary markets.

Boundary: Government bonds are not absolutely safe; inflation, default, FX and rate risk cause real losses, and market depth and investor structure affect crisis liquidity.

commodity markets

Definition: Commodity markets trade physical goods and derivatives, including crops, metals, energy and softs.

Mechanism: Spot, forward and futures markets link producers and consumers; warehouses, quality, transport and weather affect prices and basis.

Example: For example, Chicago grain futures provide hedging and price discovery for farmers and millers.

Boundary: Commodity markets are not pure speculation; financialisation, index funds and geopolitics change prices, and monopoly, hoarding and weather cause volatility beyond supply-demand.

futures and options

Definition: Futures are standardised contracts for future delivery, and options grant a right to buy or sell for a premium, used for hedging, speculation and price discovery.

Mechanism: Margin, daily marking, delivery and exercise manage credit; basis, volatility and time value determine hedge and strategy results.

Example: For example, a farmer sells futures to lock grain prices, and an option buyer pays a premium for downside protection.

Boundary: Derivatives are not free insurance; leverage, basis, counterparty and liquidity risk cause losses, complex strategies hide tails, and weak regulation amplifies systemic risk.

funds

Definition: Funds pool investor money and invest through professional managers, including mutual, pension, hedge and sovereign funds.

Mechanism: Funds lower barriers through diversification, expertise and scale; fees, governance, liquidity and capacity affect net returns.

Example: For example, 20th-century mutual funds let households diversify into stocks, and pension funds became large institutional investors.

Boundary: Funds are not return guarantees; fees, manager incentives, style drift and redemptions cause losses, and large funds can crowd, bureaucratise and drift.

securitisation and digital finance

Definition: Securitisation packages loan or rent cash flows into tradable securities, and digital finance uses internet, data and blockchain for payments, credit and investment.

Mechanism: Securitisation tranches, credit-enhances and funds through capital markets, spreading and also levering risk; digital finance lowers barriers but adds data, platform and regulatory risk.

Example: For example, mortgage securitisation turned loans into MBS, and mobile payments and P2P changed payments and credit.

Boundary: Securitisation does not remove risk; asset deterioration, rating failures and maturity mismatch caused systemic crisis, and digital finance carries fraud, privacy and monopoly risk, so innovation is not safety.

Data and institutional check: joint-stock company

Before using "joint-stock company" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns stock exchange.

Third, the institutional boundary. Buttonwood Agreement is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: joint-stock company

Learning "joint-stock company" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "stock exchange": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "Buttonwood Agreement" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of joint-stock company still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind stock exchange reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "joint-stock company" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "stock exchange" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.

Exercises

  1. What did the 1792 agreement and the 1817 organisation each solve?
  2. Why did joint-stock companies survive the South Sea Bubble?
  3. Can the 1929 decline alone prove that margin trading caused the crash?
Suggested answer points
  1. The 1792 agreement set a minimum commission and reduced price competition among brokers; the 1817 organisation fixed a meeting place and formal membership rules.
  2. Companies could spread risk and raise long-term capital; railways, banks and overseas trade still needed them.
  3. No. The decline is an outcome; leverage, bank credit, policy errors and fundamentals must be separated, and correlation is not causation.
