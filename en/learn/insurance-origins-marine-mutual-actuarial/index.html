The Origins of Insurance: Marine Insurance, Mutual Aid and Actuarial Science

Insurance did not begin with risk aversion but with maritime merchants sharing losses on ships and cargo. Marine insurance, mutual societies and life annuities turned random losses into priceable premiums; actuarial science made mortality and the law of large numbers commercial foundations.

Problem and scope

Insurance spreads individual random losses across many people. It needs three conditions: definable losses, estimable probabilities and enough participants.

Marine insurance emerged in the Mediterranean and Italian city-states: merchants divided cargo and shared losses from shipwrecks; mutual societies pooled contributions for death, illness and fire; life annuities turned mortality into a pricing basis.

Actuarial science uses life tables, the law of large numbers and compound interest to set premiums and reserves; it turned insurance from mutual agreement into an underwritable, regulated commercial contract[1][2].

Worked example

Suppose 1,000 ships, 20 total losses a year and 1 million yuan per ship. Pure premium = 20×100/1,000 = 20,000 yuan per ship; with 30% for administration and capital buffer, the premium is about 26,000.

If 30 ships are lost, claims are 30 million and premiums 26 million, a 4 million gap covered by capital or reinsurance. If only 10 are lost, premiums produce a surplus and reserves.

The law of large numbers reduces relative volatility but not systemic risk (typhoons, war); reinsurance and capital buffers handle tails. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Background

Marine insurance took shape in Italian city-state commercial contracts; Lloyd's coffee house brought shipowners, merchants and underwriters together in seventeenth-century London; Graunt estimated mortality from bills in 1662 and Halley built a life table in 1693, turning mutual promises into priced contracts. Life insurance, social insurance and reinsurance expanded the pool to national scale in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Primary materials and editions

Sources include Genoese and Venetian marine policies, the French Ordonnance de la Marine (1681), Lloyd's underwriting records, Graunt's Observations on the Bills of Mortality and Halley's life table, early life-office policies and actuarial reports, and social-insurance legislation. Exclusions and rates reveal how underwriters understood risk; brochures alone hide disputes and denied claims.

Change and boundaries

Boundary 1: insurance versus gambling. Insurance hedges an existing risk; gambling creates a new one. Insurance requires insurable interest; gambling does not.

Boundary 2: mutual versus commercial insurance. Mutual societies rely on member agreements and community trust; commercial insurers rely on actuarial science, capital and regulation. Both share risk.

Boundary 3: adverse selection and moral hazard. High-risk people buy more cover and the insured may relax precautions; underwriting, deductibles and co-insurance mitigate.

The history is not about who invented insurance but how institutions turned random losses into calculable, tradable and regulated contracts[3][4].

Boundaries and mistakes

Boundary 1: insurance is not investment. Its purpose is protection, not return; savings products contain investment components but differ in fees and liquidity.

Boundary 2: the law of large numbers is not universal. It needs independent, identically distributed risks; war, pandemics and climate change create correlation, and tails still need reinsurance.

Boundary 3: regulation and capital. Insurers need solvency capital, reserves and reinsurance; regulation protects policyholders but not every risk is insurable.

Mistake 1: cheaper insurance is better. Low premiums may mean low cover, high deductibles or exclusions; read terms and solvency.

Mistake 2: actuaries predict every loss. They predict distributions, not individual events; individual claims remain random.

Historiographical debate

Three disputes recur: where insurance ends and gambling begins, and how insurable interest is defined; whether mutual or stock organisation better controls adverse selection and moral hazard; and how actuarial fairness (risk-based pricing) balances with solidarity (cross-subsidy). Early marine practice shows that insurance needs both actuarial science and commercial networks with enforceable contracts - neither alone suffices.

Legacy and influence: marine insurance, mutuals and actuarial science

Marine insurance grew from trade-risk sharing. General average in antiquity required beneficiaries to share the loss of sacrificed cargo; medieval Mediterranean sea loans and insurance contracts transferred ship and cargo risk to insurers. Insurance was not charity but priced risk trading.

Mutuals pooled members' contributions and shared claims; early guilds and friendly societies provided death, sickness and burial benefits. Mutuals offered trust and low cost but suffered small pools, adverse selection and limited management capacity.

Actuarial science turned insurance from experience into pricing. Mortality tables, interest theory and probability made life insurance and annuities priceable; Petty, Graunt and Halley laid the demographic foundations.

Regulation followed scale: reserves, solvency, contract disclosure and consumer protection. Insurance became a quasi-public good because failure spills over to policyholders and the financial system.

Insurance and the welfare state: social insurance (pensions, health, unemployment) extended the mutual logic to the state; Bismarck's Germany is an early case. The public-private boundary depends on pool size, redistribution and political choice.

Common misuses: treating insurance as savings; treating premiums as 'waste'; ignoring adverse selection and moral hazard. Insurance is risk pooling, actuarial pricing and contract, not 'a loss if nothing happens'.

Cases: life insurance, social insurance and reinsurance

Life insurance expanded from death cover to savings and annuities; 19th-century industrial insurance offered small burial benefits to workers, with high collection costs but reach beyond traditional mutuals.

Social insurance: Bismarck's 1880s reforms created sickness, accident and pension insurance; the UK's 1942 Beveridge report designed the post-war welfare system. Social insurance widened the pool to the nation at the cost of fiscal commitments and political cycles.

Reinsurance: Lloyd's and Munich Re spread catastrophe risk to global capital markets; reinsurance cuts primary insurers' tail risk but adds counterparty and model risk.

Regulatory failures: insurer insolvencies (such as some UK life insurers in the 1990s) drove solvency regulation; AIG's CDS business in 2008 showed insurance logic applied to financial guarantees can create systemic risk.

Comparison: from marine trade to life, social insurance and reinsurance, the core remains pooling, actuarial pricing and contract; at scale, regulation and capital become necessary.

Hindsight and analogy

Explaining insurance as innate risk aversion is retrospective. Early marine insurance was closer to merchants' loss-sharing and credit arrangements; actuarial science matured later. Another bias reads modern life products back into mutual societies and ignores their community governance and religious functions. The key is not a product but how three conditions - definable loss, estimable probability and a large enough pool - were met.

Insurance-history checklist

When reading insurance history or comparing products, check five things:

  1. Is the loss definable and measurable? Marine loss, death, illness and liability have different boundaries; vaguer definitions bring more disputes and denied claims.
  2. Is the pool large enough and independent? The law of large numbers needs sample size and non-correlation; wars, pandemics and storms make losses coincide.
  3. Where do actuarial assumptions come from? Life tables, morbidity, lapse and expense assumptions set premiums; historical data can fail in a new environment.
  4. How are adverse selection and moral hazard mitigated? Underwriting, deductibles, co-insurance and waiting periods are tools; excessive screening undermines inclusion.
  5. How do mutual and commercial governance differ? Mutuals rely on member contracts and community trust; insurers on capital and regulation; who absorbs the last loss differs.

The core of insurance history is not product names but how the five conditions of insurability were met, avoided or regulated.

What would change the conclusion

Insurance-origin conclusions depend on insurability and governance:

  1. Actuarial data are thin. If mortality, morbidity and loss distributions are poorly measured, premiums rely on experience loadings and insurance is closer to a mutual promise.
  2. Risks are highly correlated. If war, plague and storms make losses coincide, the law of large numbers fails and reinsurance and capital buffers become decisive.
  3. Mutual governance is stronger. If community monitoring and member contracts control adverse selection and moral hazard, the commercial advantage shrinks.
  4. The state enters. If social insurance carries most risk, the commercial-origin story cannot explain modern protection.

Practical implication: insurance history shows insurability depends on definable loss, estimable probability and a large enough independent pool; missing one turns the product into mutual aid or gambling.

Reading supplement

Insurance history also traces insurability. Marine insurance covered a single voyage, mutual societies covered community risk, and actuarial science turned mortality and the law of large numbers into priced long-term contracts. Each expansion required independent risk, measurable loss and control of adverse selection and moral hazard; missing one pushes risk back to mutual aid or self-insurance.

Chapter takeaways

  • Marine insurance grew from trade-risk sharing, with general average as an early form.
  • Mutuals offered trust and low cost but suffered small pools, adverse selection and limited management.
  • Actuarial science turned insurance from experience into pricing, based on mortality tables and probability.
  • At scale, reserves, solvency and consumer protection became necessary regulation.
  • Insurance is risk pooling, actuarial pricing and contract, not 'a loss if nothing happens'.
