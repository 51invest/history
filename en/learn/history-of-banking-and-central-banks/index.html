A History of Banking and Central Banks

From Stockholms Banco to the Bank Charter Act and the creation of the Federal Reserve, this article covers the verifiable milestones, changing functions and early constraints of banking and central-bank institutions.

Problem and scope

This article examines commercial and central banking from the seventeenth to the twentieth century, focusing on note issuance, lender-of-last-resort support and monetary policy, with examples from Sweden, Britain and the United States. It is not a complete financial history or a guide to current regulation.

Background

Banks connect savers and borrowers through deposits, discounts and loans. Deposits are payable on demand while loans mature later; this maturity transformation eases trade but leaves banks exposed to runs. Early banks combined note issuance, exchange and government finance, and central banking functions separated only gradually.

Primary materials

Bank charters, balance sheets, notes and bills, shareholder records and official central bank histories are the main materials; statutes such as the Federal Reserve Act supply legal mandates. Official histories and legal texts still contain retrospective labels such as "oldest central bank".

Change

Stockholms Banco was founded in 1656 and issued Europe's first banknotes in 1661; it failed after overissuing. In 1668 the Swedish parliament established a successor bank, today's Sveriges Riksbank, which calls itself the world's oldest central bank (Sveriges Riksbank).

The Bank of England was founded in 1694 as a private bank to finance the government and issue notes. The Bank Charter Act of 1844 formalised note issuance and restricted new issuers; after the Overend Gurney crisis of 1866, debate over lender-of-last-resort support shaped the Bank's role; it was nationalised in 1946 (Bank of England).

In the United States, the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 established the Federal Reserve System as the central bank and set out its purposes and functions to provide a safer, more flexible and more stable monetary and financial system (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System).

Debates

First, "oldest central bank" is a retrospective label: the Riksbank’s own history page records that “The Bank of the Estates of the Realm – today Sveriges Riksbank – was established and so the world's oldest central bank was born.” [2]; that its functions differed from today's central-bank functions is this article's own reading of that passage, not a sentence taken from the source. Second, the Bank of England was privately owned until 1946, showing that public functions and private ownership can coexist. Third, dispersed note issuance may be competitive or prone to overissue; a note-issuance monopoly and lender-of-last-resort support reduce panic but create moral hazard. Fourth, central banks must be insulated from short-term political pressure while remaining accountable to legislatures.

Legacy

The modern system has two tiers: commercial banks take deposits and lend, while the central bank supplies reserves, settlement and emergency liquidity. Note issuance, lender-of-last-resort support, supervision and monetary policy are core functions, though their mix varies by country. The two-tier summary rests on the three sentences registered verbatim from source 1: a private bank founded in 1694 to act as banker to the Government and today the UK's central bank; the policy debate after Overend Gurney, which shaped its lender-of-last-resort role; and the current mission of maintaining monetary and financial stability. The two-tier phrasing itself is this article's own summary, not a quotation from the source [1].

Hindsight

From today's vantage point, it is easy to read seventeenth-century banks as proto-central banks and note monopolies as inevitable. In fact central banks formed through war finance, crisis rescue and legislative compromise, and many functions came later than the institutions.

Knowledge-point review

merchant banking and central banking

Definition: Merchant banks provided deposits, credit, exchange and trade finance, while central banks issue money, manage reserves, supervise banks and act as lender of last resort.

Mechanism: Merchant banks and bill markets supported long-distance trade, and central banks evolved from state finance and banking systems, with the Bank of England founded in 1694.

Example: For example, the Bank of England initially borrowed for the state and later took on note issue and systemic stability.

Boundary: Central banking is not ancient; its functions expanded through crises and war, and independence, political pressure and regulatory boundaries remain contested.

payments

Definition: Payments transfer money and claims through cash, cheques, bills, cards, clearing systems and cross-border networks.

Mechanism: Payments depend on accounts, clearing, settlement and credit; technology cuts costs but fraud, cyber risk and cross-border regulation persist.

Example: For example, cheque clearing and card networks enabled remote payment, and SWIFT links cross-border bank messaging.

Boundary: Payments are not neutral pipes; infrastructure, fees and access determine inclusion, outages, fraud and sanctions have wide effects, and innovation creates regulatory arbitrage.

insurance history

Definition: Insurance pools risk, charges premiums and pays claims for marine, fire, life and liability risks, an early financial intermediary.

Mechanism: Marine insurance and Lloyd's coffee house became a specialist market, life insurance and actuarial science used mortality tables, and reinsurance spread catastrophes.

Example: For example, Lloyd's brought underwriters together to share ship and cargo risk, and actuarial methods made long-term life cover priceable.

Boundary: Insurance is not simple betting; adverse selection, moral hazard, regulation and capital determine viability, and early insurance faced fraud, insolvency and discrimination.

social security and changing intermediaries

Definition: Social security provides old-age, health, unemployment and poverty support through taxes or contributions, while intermediaries expanded from banks and insurers to funds, securitisation and fintech.

Mechanism: The Beveridge report and postwar welfare states expanded social security, pension funds, mutual funds and securitisation changed saving and finance, and fintech lowered barriers with new risks.

Example: For example, postwar Britain built the NHS and pension systems, and US ERISA in the 1970s spurred retirement funds.

Boundary: Social security is not just cost; ageing, fiscal sustainability and intergenerational fairness are contested, and intermediation can add leverage, mismatch and regulatory gaps.

Data and institutional check: banknotes

Before using "banknotes" on real data, check four things. First, the unit: is the source reporting a level, a growth rate, an index or a share, and is the denominator output, population, labour force or financial assets? A sentence that changes its denominator can reverse its conclusion. Second, the time convention: nominal and real values, seasonally adjusted and unadjusted series, and fiscal versus calendar years cannot be spliced without a stated base period and conversion method. This matters most when the claim concerns lender of last resort.

Third, the institutional boundary. fractional reserves is defined by a particular legal, supervisory and accounting regime; another regime may not cover the same entities, so a cross-country comparison must first show that one rule binds the same sample. Fourth, run a reverse check: recompute the key ratio from an independent source and confirm at least the order of magnitude, direction and unit before discussing causation.

These four checks do not prove the conclusion, but they block the common failures: dividing two numbers with different definitions, treating a short swing as a long trend, and relabelling an institutional difference as a behavioural one. All teaching numbers in the article are assumptions; for formal citation, follow the date, unit and footnotes in the original source.

Boundary conditions and transfer exercises: banknotes

Learning "banknotes" should not stop at a definition. The real exercise is to separate it from "lender of last resort": the first answers which variable is moving, the second answers through which channel it affects the outcome. They often appear together, but one describes a state and the other a transmission; writing transmission as a state turns correlation into causation. The difference from "fractional reserves" is one of level: the first can be observed in a single entity, the latter requires a common rule binding the same sample. Mix the three layers and an article can appear comprehensive while testing nothing.

Boundary conditions should be observable thresholds, not adjectives. First, the sample threshold: does the data cover a full cycle, include stress periods, and include failures that exited the market? Second, the unit threshold: levels, growth rates, ratios and indices are not interchangeable, and the denominator must be stated. Third, the institutional threshold: are the effective date, covered entities and exemptions the same? Fourth, the behavioural threshold: when the metric changes, do agents actually adjust decisions, or does only the accounting number move? If any one fails, the conclusion is a scenario, not a baseline.

Transfer practice changes three dimensions, one at a time. Change the entity: from firm to household, from bank to fund; do the numerator and denominator of banknotes still hold? Change the time: from quarterly to annual, from normal to crisis; are the parameters stable? Change the institution: under another regime, does the incentive behind lender of last resort reverse? If the same mechanism survives all three, the concept is learned; if it collapses on the first change, what was remembered is a case, not a concept.

End with a self-test: explain "banknotes" in 100 words, including one unit, one time convention and one counterexample; then explain in 100 words when "lender of last resort" fails. If either is missing, return to the module parent and the sibling main article and align definitions and conventions first. Module depth is not word count; it is the ability to give a checkable judgement after changing entity, time and institution.
