From Coin to Central Bank: A History of Banking, Insurance and Payments

Modern financial intermediaries were not designed in one go. Coin, bills, deposits, insurance and pensions solved different problems in different eras: payment, long-distance settlement, risk pooling and old-age support. Central banks acquired note issue, lender-of-last-resort and supervisory functions through crises and wars.

Problem and scope

This article covers financial intermediation from early coin and bills to twentieth-century central banking, focusing on three lines: payment and settlement, risk pooling (insurance), and intertemporal support (pensions). It does not present the central bank as an ancient institution, nor one country’s experience as a universal template[1].

Background

Long-distance trade needed portable, verifiable payment; bills and deposits gradually replaced large coin shipments. Insurance grew from mutual arrangements in maritime trade, and pensions from guild, church and state obligations. Industrialisation and war expanded these needs and exposed runs and payment interruptions[2].

Primary materials and editions

The Bank of England’s official history is used for its 1694 founding and early functions; the Riksbank history for its 1668 founding; the 1913 Federal Reserve Act for the Fed’s statutory frame. All three are institutional histories or legal texts, not independent academic evaluations; dates and functions follow the originals[3].

Change and timeline

In the seventeenth century, deposit banks, bill discounting and government borrowing in Amsterdam and London combined into the prototype of modern commercial banking. The Riksbank was founded in 1668 and the Bank of England in 1694, both initially financing government and later taking on note issue and payment stability. In the nineteenth century, actuarial insurance and reinsurance developed; in the twentieth, pensions moved from voluntary mutual aid to national systems. The Federal Reserve Act of 1913 put lender-of-last-resort and note issue in one federal frame.

Debates

The first debate is the origin of central banks: a government financing tool or a product of market crises? The second is insurance and welfare: should risk pooling be market, mutual or state? The third is the regulatory boundary: does a lender of last resort encourage risk-taking? Each debate concerns institutional purpose and incentives, not just names.

Legacy and influence

Today’s payment systems, deposit insurance, reinsurance, pension accounts and central-bank liquidity tools trace back to these historical experiments. They answer three questions: who bears risk, who provides liquidity, and who guarantees last resort in a crisis.

Hindsight and analogy

Reading central-bank history as “ever-expanding government intervention” or “spontaneous market order” is hindsight bias. A safer reading is that countries chose different institutional bundles through war, trade and crisis. Comparing them requires fiscal capacity, legal tradition and market depth.

Reading and review

From coin to central bank, the key change is who stands behind money's credit. Coin value rested on metal content and sovereign credit; bills shifted payment from coin to transferable claims; banks created credit through deposits and loans and brought runs; insurance and pensions institutionalised long-term promises; the lender-of-last-resort role connected bank credit to state credit in crisis. Read this history through payment finality, reserve arrangements and crisis responsibility, not just the dates when institutions got their names. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "From Coinage to Central Banking: The History of Banking, Insurance and Payments". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Coin, bills and payments". Column two states the transmission channel of "Banking, insurance and pensions". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Central banks and crisis origins". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Banking, insurance and pensions" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Central banks and crisis origins is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Boundary case: A bank with 100 of liquidity and a 30 withdrawal: what coverage remains?

A bank holds 100 of cash and central-bank reserves against 200 of demand deposits. If 30 is withdrawn, remaining liquidity is 70 and coverage, measured as liquidity divided by remaining deposits, is 70 ÷ 170 = 41.2%. If only 10 is withdrawn, coverage is 90 ÷ 190 = 47.4%. If the bank also has 60 of quick-sale securities subject to a 20% haircut, it can add 48, for total liquidity of 118 and coverage of 69.4% of 170.

The conclusion reverses when a central bank lends as lender of last resort: if it accepts eligible collateral, the bank need not fire-sale assets and the self-fulfilling run weakens; if the central bank demands a high haircut, protection is limited. The boundary is that coin, notes, deposits, insurance and central banking are payment and risk tools from different eras. Judging safety by “enough reserves” misses maturity mismatch and the speed of a run.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not bank statements.

Bank safety also depends on asset quality and funding stability. High liquidity coverage does not help if loans are concentrated in property or highly leveraged borrowers and credit losses erode capital; deposits may look stable, but a concentrated client base can accelerate a run. Liquidity, capital and asset quality belong in the same table.
