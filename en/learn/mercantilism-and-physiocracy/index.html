Mercantilism and Physiocracy

Mercantilism and physiocracy both asked where wealth comes from and what the state should do, but answered differently; this article compares their contexts, texts, criticisms and later misreadings.

Problem and scope

Mercantilism and physiocracy are often reduced to a symmetrical pair: one favours commerce, the other agriculture. This article compares European mercantilist policies from the sixteenth to the eighteenth century with the French physiocratic school of the eighteenth century. It asks three questions: where does wealth come from, what should the state do, and how were both criticised later? It is not a complete policy history and does not treat either body of thought as a modern, unified theory. The "sixteenth to eighteenth century" framing is this article's own periodisation; no retrieved page states it.

Background

Mercantilism emerged in a Europe of competing nation-states, war and expanding overseas trade. Gold and silver served as international means of payment, while trading companies, colonies and shipping interests pressed governments to use tariffs, monopolies, navigation laws and export bounties to strengthen the realm. It was more a bundle of policies and pamphlet arguments than a textbook doctrine.

Physiocracy emerged in eighteenth-century France. Agriculture remained the dominant sector, and taxation and regulation weighed on land income. The school's two heads and their dates are stated plainly by Ingram's history: “The heads of the physiocratic school were Francois Quesnay (1694–1774) and Jean Claude Marie Vincent, sieur de Gournay (1712–1759)”, and the same page says Quesnay “was physician to Louis XV, and resided in the palace at Versailles” [2]. The older claim that he “turned to economic writing in the 1750s” is still not sourced here and is not asserted.

Primary texts

Thomas Mun's England's Treasure by Forraign Trade is a frequently cited mercantilist text. Smith names it twice: "The title of Mun's book, England's Treasure in Foreign Trade, became a fundamental maxim in the political economy, not of England only, but of all other commercial countries." and "Mr Mun compares this operation of foreign trade to the seed-time and harvest of agriculture."[1]. Mun's life, writing date and publication history are background in this article's own words; "written around 1630 and published posthumously in 1664" is not presented as a verified primary-text fact.

Quesnay's Tableau économique is not an easy textbook. Ingram says it "met with little general favour" because of its dryness and abstract form, yet "may be considered the principal manifesto of the school", and records it as published in 1758 together with the Maximes générales [2]. Encyclopaedia Britannica likewise dates it to 1758 and describes a diagram of payment flows between economic classes and sectors [3]. It divides society into a productive agricultural class, landowners, and a non-productive class of artisans and merchants, using expenditure flows to show how the surplus circulates. Ingram groups proprietors and cultivators as "the productive classes" and calls manufacturers and merchants "the unproductive class", which differs from the three labels used here; the article therefore does not claim to reproduce the original table. Its object was a particular agrarian economy, not a universal model.

Smith criticised the mercantile system in Book IV for confusing wealth with gold and silver, and also discussed the physiocratic system that treated land produce as the main source of wealth. On the navigation system he writes: "The defence of Great Britain, for example, depends very much upon the number of its sailors and shipping." "The act of navigation, therefore, very properly endeavours to give the sailors and shipping of Great Britain the monopoly of the trade of their own country, in some cases, by absolute prohibitions, and in others, by heavy burdens upon the shipping of foreign countries."[1] That is, he treats the Navigation Act as a defence measure, not merely a trade reward. Whether he separately analysed the physiocratic system could not be located in this edition, so only the sentences above are registered.

Main debates

First, was mercantilism a coherent doctrine? Most studies treat it as a collection of policies and arguments whose goals included state power, war finance and shipping. The claim that mercantilists saw trade as zero-sum does not fit Mun’s focus on the overall balance. Second, how should the policies be judged? Protecting domestic industry and shipping may bring short-term benefits, but consumers and colonies bear costs, and not every country can run a surplus at once. Third, physiocracy's premise that only agriculture produces value was criticised: the same history calls it “the erroneous opinion held by the physiocrats of the eighteenth century, that agriculture alone ... is truly productive” [2], and judges that “they condemned too absolutely the economic action of the State, both in principle and in its historic manifestations, and pushed the ‘laissez faire’ doctrine beyond its just limits” [2]. It also notes that the form of government preferred by Quesnay and his chief followers was “a legal despotism” embracing both legislative and executive function [2] — so reading laissez-faire as anti-statism misses that layer. The net-product doctrine and the tax demand appear in the same passage: “‘Laissez faire, laissez passer’ should therefore be the motto of Governments”, and state revenue “ought to be raised in the most direct and simplest way, namely, by a single impost of the nature of a land tax” [2]. The circular-flow value of the Tableau and the dispute over whether mercantilism is zero-sum are still this article's own synthesis; this source does not carry them.

Legacy

Mercantilist echoes appear in protectionism, industrial policy and trade-surplus politics. Physiocracy left traces in circular-flow analysis, laissez-faire and the study of taxation. Modern economics no longer treats gold or land as the sole source of wealth. The attribution of laissez-faire to the physiocrats now has a readable source: the history states that “‘Laissez faire, laissez passer’ should therefore be the motto of Governments” [2]. Priority is stated by the same history: Gournay “directed his efforts to the assertion and vindication of the principle of industrial liberty, and it was by him that this principle was formulated in the phrase, since so often heard for good and for evil, ‘Laissez faire et laissez passer’” [2]. That is a 1915 secondary judgement rather than contemporary evidence, so the article records the attribution as Ingram's statement and does not claim first-hand documentation of who used the phrase first.

Hindsight

Portraying mercantilists as foolish bullion worshippers ignores the world of war, metallic money and state-building they inhabited. Portraying physiocracy as a mere cult of agriculture ignores its roots in French farming and taxation. Judging past policy by today’s free-trade consensus can likewise erase the institutional and military-fiscal constraints of the time.

Knowledge-point review

mercantilism and physiocracy

Definition: Mercantilism sought national power through trade surpluses, bullion accumulation and state intervention, while physiocracy held that agriculture alone produced a net surplus.

Mechanism: Mercantilism focused on trade, colonies and manufacturing protection; physiocracy on land, natural order and simplified taxation; both addressed national wealth.

Example: For example, Colbert promoted French manufacturing and tariffs, and Quesnay used the Tableau Economique to trace agricultural circulation.

Boundary: Mercantilism is not one theory; policies mixed security, revenue and trade, and physiocracy had contested land-tax claims, so it is not simply bullion worship.

Adam Smith

Definition: Adam Smith was an 18th-century Scottish Enlightenment economist who emphasised division of labour, market exchange and limited government in The Wealth of Nations.

Mechanism: He used division of labour to explain productivity and the invisible hand to describe unintended order, while stressing institutions, law and morality.

Example: For example, pin-factory division of labour raises output, and market size makes specialisation possible.

Boundary: Smith was not an anarcho-capitalist; he supported public goods, defence and education and warned against monopoly and merchant politics, so the invisible hand is not omnipotent.

David Ricardo

Definition: David Ricardo was a 19th-century British economist known for comparative advantage, rent, money and trade theory.

Mechanism: Comparative advantage shows mutual gain from specialisation by relative cost even if one country is absolutely less productive; rent theory analyses land fertility and distribution.

Example: For example, Portugal making wine and Britain making cloth can raise total output through relative-cost exchange.

Boundary: Comparative advantage is not static destiny; technology, transport, institutions and adjustment costs change gains, trade creates distributional conflict, and jobs and transition matter.

Thomas Malthus

Definition: Thomas Malthus was an 18th-19th century English clergyman and economist known for population theory and effective demand.

Mechanism: Geometric population growth versus arithmetic food growth implied checks from famine, disease and moral restraint; he also discussed deficient demand and crises.

Example: For example, when population outruns farm output, real wages fall and crises check population.

Boundary: Malthus was not simply a pessimistic prophet; technology broke some limits, demographic transition and institutions invalidated the model, but resource-population tension remains relevant.

John Stuart Mill and industrialisation

Definition: John Stuart Mill was a 19th-century British philosopher and economist who synthesised classical economics and addressed utilitarianism, liberty, women's rights and distribution.

Mechanism: Mill separated production laws from distribution choices and discussed the stationary state, cooperatives and reform; industrialisation brought cities, factories and labour problems.

Example: For example, On Liberty argues the bounds of individual freedom, and Principles of Political Economy discusses distribution and the stationary state.

Boundary: Mill was not a simple utilitarian; he revised Bentham and valued quality and liberty, classical economics was not unified, and growth narratives often hide industrial social costs.

Exercises

  1. Sources of wealth. Where did Mun and Quesnay each locate wealth, and what did each overlook?
  2. Test the summary. Why is the statement “mercantilism banned all imports” wrong? Use the balance-of-trade argument to explain.
  3. An apparent contradiction. How could physiocrats favour laissez-faire while recognising only agriculture as productive? Are the two claims inconsistent?
Suggested answer points
  1. Mun stressed increasing gold and silver through an overall trade surplus; Quesnay stressed the net product of agriculture. Mun tended to neglect domestic consumers and long-run productivity; Quesnay tended to neglect manufacturing, services and the division of labour.
  2. Late mercantilism distinguished individual transactions from the national balance; Mun even defended exporting specie to buy goods for resale. Policy usually meant selective tariffs, monopolies and bounties, not a ban on every import.
  3. They are not inconsistent. Laissez-faire applied to grain trade and domestic regulation, while the land-only theory of production grounded the claim for a single tax on land. The premise was later criticised, but the policy position followed from the model.
