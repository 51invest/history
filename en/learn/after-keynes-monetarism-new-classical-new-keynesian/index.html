After Keynes: Monetarism, New Classical and New Keynesian Economics

Keynes brought effective demand, uncertainty and policy space into macroeconomics; postwar debates turned on how potent fiscal policy is, how much money matters, and how expectations change rules. Monetarism, new classical and new Keynesian economics are not three slogans but three models of shocks, expectations and policy response.

Problem and scope

This article covers the main macro debates from the Keynesian revolution of the 1930s to new Keynesian economics in the 1980s: whether fiscal policy can stabilise aggregate demand, whether money matters more than fiscal policy, and whether expectations can neutralise policy. It compares model structures rather than treating “Keynesianism” as one party programme[1].

Background

The Great Depression broke classical self-correction: output fell, unemployment rose, and falling prices did not quickly restore investment. Keynes’s General Theory (1936) argued that effective demand could be insufficient and that public spending and monetary easing could fill the gap. Postwar Fordism, Bretton Woods and welfare-state expansion made demand management routine[2].

Primary materials and editions

Keynes’s earlier Tract on Monetary Reform (1923) is used for his early view on inflation and money; the General Theory is the 1936 text; Blinder’s entry is used to check core Keynesian claims; Bryan’s Federal Reserve history is used for the policy background of the 1970s Great Inflation. The article does not equate the General Theory with all Keynesianism, nor monetarism with all of Friedman’s work[3].

Change and timeline

Postwar Keynesianism merged with IS-LM into “fiscal first, money second” demand management. The 1970s stagflation exposed the fragility of the Phillips curve: inflation and unemployment could rise together, and expansionary policy no longer stabilised. Monetarism stressed money-supply rules and long-run neutrality; new classical macro used rational expectations to show that expectations can offset policy; new Keynesian economics kept sticky prices and nominal frictions to explain why short-run policy still works.

Debates

The first debate is the fiscal multiplier: does public spending crowd out private investment or fill a demand gap? The second is rules versus discretion: do fixed rules reduce uncertainty or make policy rigid? The third is expectations: do households and firms adjust in advance, so policy changes prices but not output? Each debate needs different identification and data.

Legacy and influence

After Keynes, macroeconomics split into demand management, monetary rules and expectations-with-frictions lines. Central-bank independence, inflation targeting, automatic stabilisers and macroprudential policy are institutional deposits of these debates. The 2008 and 2020 crises brought fiscal multipliers, unconventional monetary policy and supply shocks back to the centre.

Hindsight and analogy

Reading Keynes as “government always spends more” or monetarism as “just print money” is hindsight bias. A safer reading is that schools differ in their assumptions about shock sources, expectation responses and policy lags; policy effects depend on whether those assumptions hold. Using a 1936 model on a 2026 supply-chain shock requires checking shock type and institutions.

Reading and review

Post-Keynesian debates look like schools but turn on three assumptions: whether shocks are demand or supply, how fast prices and expectations adjust, and how long policy lags last. Monetarism trusts rules and stable money, new classical economics stresses expectations and equilibrium, new Keynesian economics uses sticky prices to explain short-run frictions. Judge a theory by the shock, institution and horizon under which it holds; attributing a policy success to one school often ignores fiscal space and the external environment. Public references for the institutional definitions are cited [4]; all worked numbers are teaching assumptions.

Reading checklist

A final caution is that schools are not teams. A policy can borrow a rule from monetarism, a friction from new Keynesian models and a supply shock from classical analysis in the same episode. Label the assumption, not the brand, when explaining the outcome.

Working paper: a four-column check for "After Keynes: Monetarism, New Classical and New Keynesian Economics"

The subject of this article is "After Keynes: Monetarist, Neoclassical, and New Keynes". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Effective demand and the multiplier". Column two states the transmission channel of "Monetarism, new classical and new Keynesian". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Expectations, rules and policy lags". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Monetarism, new classical and new Keynesian" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Expectations, rules and policy lags is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.

Review sentence

Write one sentence only: "Under the ____ convention, a change in ____ affects ____ through ____; if ____ appears, the claim fails." The four blanks are the unit, the variable, the channel and the falsifier. If the sentence cannot be written, at least one of the four columns is still empty; if it can be written but cannot be observed, the falsifier is wrong. On every data update, reread this sentence before reading the charts.

Boundary case: Multipliers of 1.5 and 0.8: how much of a 100 output gap can 40 close?

With an output gap of 100 and fiscal spending of 40, a multiplier of 1.5 adds 60 of output and leaves a gap of 40; a multiplier of 0.8 adds only 32 and leaves 68. If government borrowing raises interest rates by one point and private investment falls by 10, the net multiplier falls to 1.25 and output rises 50; if the central bank cuts rates and investment does not fall, the multiplier can stay at 1.5.

The conclusion reverses under expectations and supply constraints: if households expect tax increases or firms have no spare capacity, the spending multiplier falls; large import leakage also lowers it. The boundary is that monetarism, new classical and new Keynesian debates are not about whether fiscal policy works at all, but about multiplier size, expectations and policy rules. Replacing model structure with one multiplier turns a debate into a slogan.

All numbers are teaching assumptions, not fiscal forecasts.
