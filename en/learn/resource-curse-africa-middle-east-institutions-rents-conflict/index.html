The Resource Curse in Africa and the Middle East: Institutions, Rents and Conflict

Resource abundance does not guarantee growth: rents concentrate, institutions get captured and conflict gets financed. Africa and the Middle East show that the resource curse is not geological destiny but a result of institutions, rent distribution and external shocks.

Problem and scope

The resource curse is the tendency of resource-rich countries to underperform in long-run growth, institutional quality and conflict risk. It is not a simple 'more resources, more poverty' law but an overlap of mechanisms.

Three mechanisms: rent concentration (mineral and oil rents flow into budgets and become contested), institutional capture (elites control rents and suppress property rights and competition), and price shocks (commodity swings force sharp fiscal and exchange-rate adjustments).

The curse is not inevitable: Norway, Chile and some Gulf states turned rents into sovereign funds and public services; institutions, transparency and diversification are key[1][2].

Worked example

Suppose oil is 70% of fiscal revenue and the price falls from 80 to 40 dollars a barrel, halving revenue. With rigid spending, deficits widen, the currency depreciates and inflation rises; cutting subsidies raises social-conflict risk.

If a sovereign fund equals two years of imports, it buffers the shock; without one, the country borrows or prints. Institutional quality decides whether rents fund education and infrastructure or patronage and the military.

Policy space depends on rent transparency, fiscal rules, sovereign funds, the exchange-rate regime and property rights. All numbers are teaching assumptions.

Background

The resource-curse debate followed the oil booms of the 1950s-70s, the Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s and African civil wars of the 1990s. Sachs and Warner (1995) found that countries with high resource exports grew more slowly; Auty (1993) coined the term. The 2000s commodity super-cycle, the EITI transparency initiative and Norway's sovereign fund supplied new comparisons.

Primary materials and editions

Main sources are World Bank and IMF fiscal and export data, the Sachs-Warner dataset, oil and mining contracts, sovereign-fund annual reports, EITI disclosures, and budget documents and conflict records for Nigeria, Norway and Chile. Measurement matters: resource rents by price or output, exports as a share of GDP or fiscal revenue, and which institutional indicator is used all change the conclusion.

Change and mechanisms

Mechanism 1: fiscal dependence. The larger resource rents are in the budget, the more price shocks transmit to budgets and stability.

Mechanism 2: institutional capture. Rents reduce the need for broad taxation, weakening accountability; elites control licences and procurement.

Mechanism 3: conflict finance. Minerals and oil fund armed groups; resource location and transport corridors affect conflict duration.

The answer is not 'leave resources in the ground' but placing rents under transparent rules, stabilisation funds and diversified economies; institutional quality is the core variable[3][4].

Boundaries and mistakes

Boundary 1: the curse is not geological determinism. Resource countries differ widely in institutions and outcomes; resources are a condition, not destiny.

Boundary 2: rents are not the only variable. Colonial legacies, ethnic structure, geography and external intervention also shape governance.

Boundary 3: the energy transition changes rents. Falling oil demand will affect budgets and geopolitics; resource countries must adjust early.

Mistake 1: abundance always causes conflict. Conflict needs political and economic conditions; resources are one financing source.

Mistake 2: a sovereign fund solves the curse. Funds need transparent governance and investment discipline; poorly accountable funds get diverted.

Historiographical debate

Three disputes stand out. Is the curse an effect of resources or a proxy for institutional quality - resource abundance may be a consequence, not a cause? Which mechanism matters more, Dutch disease or the rentier state? Are Norway and Chile exceptions of institutions or replicable policy packages? Cross-country regressions struggle with endogeneity and case comparisons with generalisation; the robust conclusion is conditional, not 'resources always curse'.

Legacy and influence: rents, institutions and conflict

The resource curse is not 'resources make a country poor'. It is a set of mechanisms: rent dependence, weakened institutions, conflict and exchange-rate appreciation. When prices are high, governments collect large rents; without checks, rents fund patronage and repression rather than education and infrastructure. When prices fall, fiscal collapse raises conflict risk.

State capture of rents is the core variable. Mineral and oil rents are easy for a central government to control, with concentrated property rights and easy taxation, which weakens the need to be accountable to taxpayers. Agricultural and manufacturing taxes need broad registration and bargaining, and are more likely to nurture representative institutions; resource rents are not.

'Booty rule' and conflict: when rents are large enough, controlling the state becomes the most profitable business; civil war, coups and separatism cluster around resource regions. Rebel financing, external intervention and transport corridors determine how long conflict lasts.

Exchange-rate appreciation (Dutch disease): resource exports bring foreign exchange, the currency strengthens and manufacturing exports lose competitiveness; if institutions cannot convert rents into productivity, the economy becomes monocultural.

Institutional quality is not fixed. Pre-discovery institutions, colonial legacies, ethnic structure and aid shape the path; some countries used sovereign funds, fiscal rules and transparency to improve outcomes (Norway, Chile), others fell into conflict (the Niger Delta, eastern Congo).

Measurement: resource rents as a share of GDP, export concentration, fiscal dependence, governance indicators and conflict events. One indicator misleads; a combination separates 'resource-rich with good governance' from 'resource-rich with weak institutions'.

Common misuses: treating the curse as geographic determinism; treating nationalisation as the only answer; treating aid as a panacea. Resources are an opportunity; institutions decide whether they become development or conflict.

Comparative cases: Norway, Chile and Nigeria

Norway had mature institutions and strong fiscal discipline before oil was found in 1969; a sovereign wealth fund isolates rents from the annual budget, fiscal rules cap spending and transparency is high. Resources did not produce good outcomes automatically; institutions came first.

Chile paired copper nationalisation with stabilisation funds, fiscal rules and an independent central bank, saving in high-price years and spending in low-price years; yet Chile remains copper-dependent with limited diversification.

Nigeria's oil rents are highly centralised; federal allocation formulas and state dependence bred corruption, poor infrastructure and Delta conflict, with producing regions' grievances entangled with central control.

The DRC's minerals intertwine with armed conflict, external supply chains and artisanal mining; resource governance is a security and human-rights problem as much as a fiscal one.

Comparative lesson: resources do not determine outcomes; pre-discovery institutions, fiscal rules, transparency and conflict management decide whether rents become development. Transplanting the Norwegian model into weak-institution states often fails.

Hindsight and analogy

Retrospective accounts turn the curse into geological destiny and ignore price cycles and policy choice. After the 2010s commodity downturn some countries borrowed again; the green transition now strands oil assets. Predicting growth from resource abundance misses institutions, transparency and fiscal rules; generalising from Norway misses small size, the sovereign fund and consensus politics.

What would change the conclusion

Resource-curse conclusions depend on the relative roles of institutions and prices:

  1. Institutions precede resources. If weak institutions existed before discovery, the curse is more an institutional outcome than a resource cause.
  2. A commodity super-cycle. If prices stay high for long, fiscal buffers and sovereign funds accumulate more easily and the curse weakens.
  3. Small states and consensus. If the country is small and consensus strong, Norway-style funds and fiscal rules are easier to sustain.
  4. Green transition. If oil assets strand, resource exporters' fiscal and exchange-rate risks are repriced and old conclusions need updating.

Practical implication: resource-country assessments need rent transparency, fiscal rules and diversification together; using Norway as a template requires asking whether size and consensus politics are comparable.

Data note: resource rents by price or output, exports as a share of GDP or fiscal revenue, change the curse reading; cite the institutional indicator source.

Scope note: label price and exchange-rate assumptions for resource rents; a change in oil price, output or fiscal definition changes the curse reading.

Chapter takeaways

  • The resource curse combines rent dependence, weak institutions, conflict and currency appreciation; it is not geographic determinism.
  • Resource rents are easy for the state to control and weaken accountability to taxpayers.
  • Dutch disease transmits through currency appreciation and manufacturing competitiveness.
  • Norway and Chile differ from Nigeria and the DRC in pre-discovery institutions and governance.
  • Resources are an opportunity; institutions decide development or conflict.
