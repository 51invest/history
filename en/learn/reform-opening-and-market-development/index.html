Reform, Opening and Market Development

From 1978 to 2023, this article examines how reform and opening widened markets and changed trade and growth, and how policy, data and interpretation should be kept apart.

ModuleHIS-M09 · 中国经济发展
LevelSystematic
Read4 min · 13 sections

Problem and scope

This article covers marketization, opening and growth from 1978 to 2023. It does not narrate the full policy history, and it does not attribute growth to any single reform.

Background

In 1978 China's GDP was 367.9 billion yuan and gross goods trade was 20.6 billion US dollars [1][4]. At the end of that year the urbanization rate was only 17.92%, and 97.5% of the rural population fell below the current poverty line [1]. On the World Bank's goods-and-services measure, trade was 15.4% of GDP [2]; the 20.6 billion dollar figure covers goods only and cannot be used to recompute 15.4%. Prices were subject to a highly centralized planning system, and price reform began with higher agricultural procurement prices [5].

Primary materials

NBS yearbooks and annual communiqués, customs statistics, the World Bank's World Development Indicators (WDI), WTO documents, and enterprise and household surveys are the main materials. Historical GDP is revised, and measured trade openness is sensitive to exchange rates and processing trade.

Change

After the Third Plenum of December 1978, reform began in the countryside with the household responsibility system [4]. Price reform proceeded step by step: agricultural procurement prices were raised first; after 1984 most agricultural and many consumer goods prices were freed, and a dual-track system was applied to industrial producer goods, which was basically abolished in 1994 [5]. Opening began with Shenzhen and other special economic zones and with coastal, border, river and inland cities [4]. In the 1990s the reform goal of a socialist market economy was established [4], and on 11 December 2001 China joined the WTO [3].

GDP rose from 367.9 billion yuan in 1978 to more than 126 trillion yuan in 2023, an average of 8.9% a year over 1979-2023; it passed 1 trillion yuan in 1986, 10 trillion in 2000 and 100 trillion in 2020 [1]. Trade, measured as exports plus imports of goods and services, rose from 15.4% of GDP in 1978 to 36.7% in 2023 [2]. China became the largest goods trader, and its 2023 GDP was about 17% of the world total [1].

Debates

First, gradual reform versus shock therapy: gradualism preserved institutional continuity; how to weigh the gains of dual-track pricing against its rent-seeking costs is a contested question. Second, export orientation versus domestic demand: the effects of openness on technology, jobs and competition, and the exposure to external demand, must be assessed for specific periods and data. Third, growth attribution: the relative contributions of markets, demography, globalization and savings are questions for growth accounting and counterfactual analysis, not grounds for crediting a single policy. Fourth, trade dependence is not a complete measure of openness, and processing trade double-counts imports and exports.

Legacy

In goods trade, China became the world's largest trading nation; in current prices, household disposable income per head rose from 171 yuan in 1978 to 39,218 yuan in 2023, and consumption per head from 151 to 26,796 yuan [1]. Income gaps, environmental costs and external dependence remain issues that require separate examination.

Hindsight

From today's scale it is easy to read reform as a predetermined success; the record shows a mixture of policy choices and macroeconomic adjustment, not an unbroken upward path.

GDP of 367.9 billion yuan in 1978, more than 126 trillion yuan in 2023, average annual growth of 8.9% over 1979-2023, and the crossings of 1 trillion, 10 trillion and 100 trillion yuan in 1986, 2000 and 2020 are taken from the National Bureau of Statistics report [1].

Exercises

  1. Does a higher trade-to-GDP ratio necessarily mean a more open market?
  2. Why should the 8.9% average growth of 1979-2023 not be extrapolated into the future?
  3. What did WTO accession mean for domestic firms and for consumers?
Suggested answer points
  1. Not necessarily. The ratio depends on economic size, exchange rates and processing trade; tariffs, market access and capital flows must also be examined.
  2. A larger base, demographic change, slower globalization and environmental constraints all lower potential growth; a historical average is not a forecast.
  3. Firms faced more competition and a larger market, while consumers gained more goods and lower prices; adjustment costs fell on some sectors and regions.
