The Evolution of Monetary Systems

From commodity money and coinage to convertible paper and fiat money, how issuers, redemption promises and credibility changed.

ModuleHIS-M11 · 货币制度史
LevelSystematic
Read6 min · 14 sections

Problem and scope

This article traces how monetary systems moved from commodity money and coinage to convertible paper and fiat money, from antiquity to the twentieth century, mainly in China and Europe. It is not a full price history and does not treat fiat money as the end point.

Background

Money began as commodities: livestock, grain, shells and metals. Metals are divisible and durable, but each transaction required weighing and assaying. Rulers reduced these costs by monopolising coinage and setting weight and fineness standards, so money came to combine commodity value, a claim to payment and state certification.

Primary materials

Coins, mint ordinances, surviving notes, bank ledgers and central bank archives are the main materials. Song-dynasty jiaozi, seventeenth-century Swedish notes and early Bank of England notes survive in texts, catalogues and account books. Paper money's value must also be checked against price and redemption records, not the note alone.

Change

In Northern Song Sichuan, copper was scarce and iron coins circulated. Iron was heavy, so merchants opened jiaozi shops, took deposits and issued convertible paper receipts. Jiaozi is treated as China's and the world's earliest paper money; the government later banned private issuance and issued official jiaozi, called qianyin (People's Bank of China, Guizhou Branch).

Stockholms Banco was founded in 1656 and issued Europe's first banknotes in 1661; it failed after issuing too many notes. In 1668 the Swedish parliament established a successor bank, today's Sveriges Riksbank, which describes itself as the world's oldest central bank (Sveriges Riksbank). The Bank of England was founded in 1694 as a private bank to finance the government; its 1697 running cash note, promising to pay the bearer, is regarded as the forerunner of the modern banknote (Bank of England).

The gold standard formalised convertibility - that page reads verbatim "The gold standard linked the value of the UK currency directly to gold, and effectively enabled people to exchange Bank of England banknotes for the equivalent value of gold." and "In September 1931, the UK suspended the gold standard."[4]. The two-world-wars weakening and the late-twentieth-century turn to fiat money are this article's summary; the pages read here never use the word faces fiat, specie, price-specie or convertib.

Debates

First, commodity-money and credit-money views stress intrinsic value and claims to payment, respectively. Second, paper money arose from both commercial and fiscal needs: China had private jiaozi before official issue, so no single cause explains it. Third, the gold standard lowered transaction costs but pushed adjustment onto wages and prices. Fourth, fiat money is not "unbacked": law, taxation, central bank independence and public expectations sustain its value.

Legacy

Central banks dominate note issuance, law defines legal tender and conversion rules, and payments settle in central bank liabilities. Cash is a central bank liability and deposits are commercial bank liabilities; the core questions remain issuance, redemption and credibility.

Hindsight

From fiat money, it is easy to narrate a necessary march from metal to paper. In fact convertibility was restored several times, and currency unions and dollarisation occurred; change was driven by crisis and political choice.

The heavy iron cash of Sichuan in the Northern Song, the merchant-issued jiaozi described as the earliest paper money in China and in the world, and the later ban on private issue followed by official notes (qianyin) follow the People’s Bank of China financial-education page [1].

Knowledge-point review

coins and paper money

Definition: Coins are metal money minted to weight and fineness, and paper money is convertible or fiat notes issued by states, banks or institutions.

Mechanism: Coin value depends on metal content, trust and minting rights; notes depend on issuer credit, reserves and legal status, and debasement and forgery destroy trust.

Example: For example, Song jiaozi began as merchant-issued convertible notes and later became state-run and depreciated under overissue.

Boundary: Paper money is not a modern invention; early notes coexisted with metal convertibility, and issuer credit and fiscal pressure determined stability, so paper is not a central bank.

gold and silver systems

Definition: Gold and silver systems use gold, silver or bimetallism as the monetary base, with fixed ratios, coinage and reserves.

Mechanism: Gold-silver ratios, supply, demand and discoveries affect money and prices; bimetallism is vulnerable to arbitrage and ratio shifts, and silver-standard countries faced FX pressure in the gold era.

Example: For example, 19th-century ratio changes moved money in and out of bimetallic countries.

Boundary: Gold and silver are not naturally stable; discoveries, war and mining change supply, bimetallic arbitrage and competitive debasement create instability, and silver peripheries were shaped by core policies.

gold standard

Definition: The gold standard fixed currencies to gold and adjusted balances through gold flows, prevailing classically from about 1870 to 1914.

Mechanism: Fixed rates, convertibility and central-bank rules constrained policy; deficits drained gold, contracting money and prices, with peripheries bearing more adjustment.

Example: For example, Britain anchored sterling to gold, shaping trade and capital-flow networks.

Boundary: The gold standard was not automatic harmony; adjustment came through unemployment and recession, central banks deviated, colonies and silver countries were excluded, and gold supply and war broke it.

Bretton Woods

Definition: Bretton Woods was a 1944 international monetary arrangement linking the dollar to gold and other currencies to the dollar, creating the IMF and World Bank.

Mechanism: Adjustable pegs, capital controls and dollar reserves supported postwar trade; US balances and gold reserves determined stability.

Example: For example, postwar Europe and Japan anchored to the dollar to rebuild trade, and widening US deficits eroded confidence.

Boundary: Bretton Woods was not global free markets; capital controls, unequal representation and adjustment burdens, and the Triffin dilemma between dollar reserves and gold broke it in 1971.

floating rates and digital money

Definition: Floating exchange rates let currency prices be set by market supply and demand, and digital money includes crypto assets, stablecoins and central bank digital currencies.

Mechanism: Floating rates absorb shocks but are volatile, and capital flows and rate differentials drive currencies; digital money changes payments, issuance and regulation.

Example: For example, major currencies floated in the 1970s, the euro started as a unit of account in 1999, and central banks now study CBDCs.

Boundary: Floating is not fully free; intervention, controls and dollarisation persist, and digital money is not a fiat substitute, with credit, regulatory and technological risks.

Exercises

  1. How did issuance and redemption differ between jiaozi and early Bank of England notes?
  2. Why is paper money not the same as fiat money?
  3. Why did gold-standard adjustment depress wages?
Suggested answer points
  1. Jiaozi was first issued by Sichuan merchants against iron deposits, later by the state; Bank of England notes were issued by a chartered bank and promised metal payment.
  2. Paper money can be a convertible banknote or an inconvertible fiat currency; the difference is issuer, redemption promise and legal status.
  3. A deficit country lost gold and its money supply contracted, so wages and prices had to fall; nominal wage rigidity cut output and employment.
