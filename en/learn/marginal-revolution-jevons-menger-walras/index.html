The Marginal Revolution: Jevons, Menger and Walras on Exchange

The marginal revolution shifted economics from “how much is produced” to “how much is one more unit worth”. Jevons, Menger and Walras almost simultaneously found that value comes from marginal utility and equilibrium from exchange ratios; Marshall then turned marginal analysis into a teachable price theory.

Problem and scope

This article examines how Jevons, Menger, Walras and Marshall brought “marginal” into value and exchange theory around the 1870s. The point is not who was first, but what each path solved: Jevons’s mathematical utility, Menger’s subjective needs, Walras’s exchange equilibrium[1].

Background

Mid-nineteenth-century economics still centred on labour value and production cost. Industrial expansion, railways and unified markets made price movements more frequent, and economists needed a tool for “why goods with similar cost sell at different prices”. Marginal utility offered a new clue: value is not an intrinsic property of a good but a person’s valuation of “one more unit”[2].

Primary materials and editions

Jevons’s Theory of Political Economy (1871); Menger’s Grundsätze (1871); Marshall’s Principles of Economics (1890, eighth edition). Their mathematical intensity differed: Jevons used utility functions, Menger used subjective needs and goods classification, Walras used general-equilibrium equations, and Marshall turned marginal analysis into a textbook combining diagrams and prose[3].

Change and timeline

Jevons wrote utility as a diminishing function of quantity and made “final utility” determine exchange value. Menger started not from equations but from human needs and the causal chain of goods, explaining that scarce goods derive value from the last use. Walras placed many goods in one equilibrium system, with prices adjusting to clear supply and demand. Marshall combined the three, using marginal cost, marginal revenue and demand curves to make price theory teachable.

Debates

The first debate is whether marginal utility explains all prices: capital goods, public goods and brand premia do not map directly to consumer utility. The second is how subjective value combines with objective cost: Marshall reconciled long-run cost and short-run demand, but the reconciliation has assumptions. The third is whether mathematical formalisation loses institutional content: the Menger tradition stresses institutions and knowledge, the Walras tradition stresses equilibrium conditions.

Legacy and influence

Marginal analysis became the base of modern microeconomics: consumer surplus, producer surplus, indifference curves and general equilibrium all extend from it. It also shifted economics from “how much is produced” to “resource allocation” and gave later welfare economics, game theory and industrial organisation their language.

Hindsight and analogy

Calling the marginal revolution a “complete overthrow of labour value” is hindsight bias. A more accurate reading is that marginal analysis offered a new price explanation, while labour, cost and institutions remained part of price formation. Using an 1871 utility function on today’s platform pricing requires checking data, market power and externalities.

Reading and review

The marginal revolution was not three people discovering one formula; it was three entrances: Jevons from exchange ratios and utility, Menger from subjective needs and the classification of goods, Walras from equilibrium equations and price clearing. They agreed on moving value from labour or cost to marginal utility but differed in method: British empirical mathematics, German institutional subjectivity, French equilibrium systems. Read the revolution through how it changed the answer to “why is a price justified”, not through three names. Working paper: four columns for a checkable judgement

The subject of this article is "Marginal Revolution: The Exchange World of Jevons, Menger and Walras". Do not begin by memorising a conclusion; begin with a four-column worksheet. Column one states the definition, numerator, denominator and unit of "Marginal utility and exchange ratios". Column two states the transmission channel of "Three founders, three paths". Column three states the institutional boundary of "Marshall and the institutionalisation of price theory". Column four states the signal that would refute the conclusion. Only when all four are filled may the conclusion travel to the next step; an empty column turns it into an assumption.

Column one is where shortcuts appear. Writing "higher is better" is not a definition. At minimum, answer whether the metric is a stock or a flow, whether the denominator is output, population, assets or transaction volume, whether the price is nominal or real, and whether the observation is an endpoint or a period average. Change any one and the ranking or conclusion can move. If a source gives a number without a denominator, return to its footnote rather than borrowing the denominator from a neighbouring article.

Column two turns "Three founders, three paths" into an observable chain: which variable moves first, through which intermediary, and which outcome changes last. Every link needs a time and a unit; do not mix "long run" with "quarterly". The longer the chain, the more important it is to mark which link is the author's inference and which link has source support. Inference may teach, but it may not masquerade as fact.

Column three handles institutions. Marshall and the institutionalisation of price theory is not background colour; it is the sample definition. Which entities are covered, which are exempt, when does the rule take effect, and does a cross-border comparison bind the same sample to one rule? Different institutions attach different incentives to the same number. Writing an institutional difference as a behavioural difference is the most common error in cross-market comparison. If the rules cannot be aligned, downgrade the comparison to two separate scenarios and do not rank them.

Column four writes the refutation. End with a falsifiable sentence: "If A is observed and C follows within B, the claim holds; otherwise it fails." A must be observable, B must be a waitable period, and C must be a checkable result. If the three cannot be written, the claim is not testable yet. Keep the worksheet, source links and review date on one page; in the next review, read the four columns before reading the outcome.

Record template

Compress the four columns into one page: measurement and unit on the left, mechanism and institution in the middle, refutation and review date on the right. When data update, change only the numbers and not the conclusion sentence; if the sentence itself must change, the measurement or the institution has changed, so return to the source and redo the check. Keep one column for the opposing view. In the next review, ask whether evidence overturned that view before asking whether the position made money.

A useful record has five fields: the source URL, the access date, the denominator, the rule's effective date and one falsifier. Five fields make the record portable: another reader can reopen the source, recompute the ratio and see which condition would have changed the judgement. A record without them is a diary, not evidence.
